## Week 2 — Why People Behave the Way They Do

*Social Psychology, Incentives & Human Motivation* · School of Behavioral & Social Science · School of Business & Leadership · 7 hours

> How much of what people do at work is explained by the situations, incentives and social expectations leaders create — and how should that change the way you design them?

**Objectives**

- Diagnose a recurring behavior problem by examining incentives, norms, status and friction before attributing it to individual character.
- Classify influential social-psychology findings as robust, context-dependent or failed to replicate, and explain what that implies for using them.
- Explain self-determination theory and the conditions under which rewards strengthen or undermine motivation.
- Apply the principal–agent and multitask models to predict effort diversion and gaming under a proposed incentive.
- Design an incentive system that integrates measure, unit, horizon, payout shape, monitoring and non-financial levers, and state its expected side effects.
- Evaluate claims about reciprocity, fairness and psychological safety by distinguishing correlational from causal evidence.

**Lessons**

1. **The Power of the Situation — and What Survived Replication** (40 min) — Behavior is jointly produced by people and the situations they are placed in, and the classic social-psychology evidence for situational power is a mix of robust, fragile and failed findings.
2. **Why People Try: Self-Determination, Rewards and Crowding Out** (40 min) — Motivation differs in kind as well as amount, and rewards can strengthen or weaken it depending on whether they feel controlling or informative and on what the work actually requires.
3. **Rewarding A, Hoping for B: Agency, Multitasking and Gaming** (45 min) — When people do several tasks that differ in how well they can be measured, strong incentives on the measured ones pull effort away from the rest and invite gaming, so incentive strength, metric choice and job design must be decided together.
4. **Norms, Reciprocity, Status and Safety: The Social Economy of Work** (40 min) — Much of what people do at work is governed not by formal incentives but by norms, reciprocity, judgments of fairness, identity and the perceived safety of speaking up — forces that formal incentives can strengthen or destroy.

**Required reading:** Gneezy et al. (2011), *When and Why Incentives (Don’t) Work to Modify Behavior* — Full article (pp. 191–210); Kerr (1975), *On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B* — Full article

**Optional reading:** Holmström et al. (1991), *Multitask Principal–Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design*; Fehr et al. (2000), *Fairness and Retaliation: The Economics of Reciprocity*; Deci et al. (1999), *A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation*; Frazier et al. (2017), *Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension*; Cerasoli et al. (2014), *Intrinsic Motivation and Extrinsic Incentives Jointly Predict Performance: A 40-Year Meta-Analysis*

## Week 3 — How Humans Learn and Develop Expertise

*Learning Science, Skill Acquisition & Deliberate Practice* · School of Behavioral & Social Science · School of Business & Leadership · 7 hours

> What actually makes people get better at difficult skills — and how would you know whether a development system is producing learning or only good-looking practice?

**Objectives**

- Distinguish learning from performance and design evaluations that measure retention and transfer rather than practice success.
- Evaluate deliberate-practice theory and its critiques, interpreting variance-explained estimates and range restriction correctly.
- Grade motor-learning principles — contextual interference, feedback frequency, attentional focus, constraints-led design — by laboratory, applied and bias-corrected evidence.
- Explain cognitive load, the limits of transfer and the learning-styles myth, and individualize instruction by expertise rather than by labels.
- Build an evidence-informed development framework that separates research-supported elements from coaching hypotheses.

**Lessons**

1. **Learning Is Not Performance: Memory, Retrieval and Desirable Difficulties** (38 min) — What you see during practice is performance; learning is what survives a delay and a change of context — and the two can move in opposite directions.
2. **Deliberate Practice and Its Critics: What Explains Expertise?** (38 min) — Deliberate practice describes what high-quality practice looks like, but the claim that accumulated practice explains most differences between people has not held up well.
3. **Motor Learning: Practice Schedules, Feedback, Attention — and a Live Debate** (42 min) — Motor-learning research offers powerful laboratory findings on practice scheduling, feedback and attention, but several of them shrink in applied settings or under bias correction, and the field’s two main theoretical camps still disagree about what is being learned.
4. **Expertise, Transfer and the Coach’s Job: Cognitive Load, Myths and Measurement** (36 min) — Experts differ from novices in what they have stored, skills transfer less widely than people hope, learning styles are not a sound basis for instruction, and a development system is only as good as its measures of learning.

**Required reading:** Soderstrom et al. (2015), *Learning Versus Performance: An Integrative Review* — Full article; read the sections on motor learning, verbal learning and metacognition closely, skim the animal-learning history.; Macnamara et al. (2016), *The Relationship Between Deliberate Practice and Performance in Sports: A Meta-Analysis* — Abstract, introduction and general discussion (an author copy is hosted on a university lab page).; Czyż et al. (2024), *The Effect of Contextual Interference on Transfer in Motor Learning: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis* — Abstract, results by setting and age, and discussion (open access).

**Optional reading:** Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology*; III et al. (2006), *Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention*; Ericsson et al. (2019), *Deliberate Practice and Proposed Limits on the Effects of Practice on the Acquisition of Expert Performance*; Macnamara et al. (2019), *The Role of Deliberate Practice in Expert Performance: Revisiting Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993)*; McKay et al. ## Week 4 — How to Know What Is True

*Research Literacy, Statistics & Causal Reasoning* · School of Behavioral & Social Science · School of Business & Leadership · 7.5 hours

> When a study, a dashboard or a trusted colleague says something works, how do you decide how much to believe it and what to do about it?

**Objectives**

- Distinguish signal from noise using regression to the mean, confidence intervals, p-values and statistical power.
- Explain how researcher degrees of freedom, low power and publication bias produce exaggerated and false findings, and evaluate remedies.
- Identify confounding, selection and collider bias and match randomized and quasi-experimental designs to causal questions.
- Critique a study’s effect size, measurement, external validity and susceptibility to metric gaming.
- Combine several imperfect lines of evidence into a stated degree of belief and a proportionate decision.

**Lessons**

1. **Signal, Noise and the Meaning of p** (35 min) — Every measurement mixes signal with noise; regression to the mean, confidence intervals, p-values and power are tools for keeping the two apart.
2. **The Replication Crisis and the Garden of Forking Paths** (35 min) — Low power, flexible analysis and selective publication combine to fill literatures with exaggerated and false findings; preregistration and registered reports are partial remedies.
3. **Causal Inference for Executives** (40 min) — Every causal claim is a comparison with a counterfactual; randomization and a handful of quasi-experimental designs make that comparison credible, each under one checkable assumption.
4. **Reading a Study Like a Skeptic** (30 min) — A credible design is not enough: you must also judge the size of the effect, the validity of the measures, the population studied, and whether the metric still means what it used to.
5. **Evidence Hierarchies, Meta-Analysis and Their Limits** (30 min) — Hierarchies of evidence are useful defaults, not verdicts; meta-analyses inherit the biases of what they combine, and good judgment weighs design, consistency, mechanism and prior knowledge together.

**Required reading:** Wasserstein et al. (2016), *The ASA’s Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose* — Full statement (about five pages); Simmons et al. (2011), *False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant* — Full article; Angrist et al. (2010), *The Credibility Revolution in Empirical Economics: How Better Research Design Is Taking the Con out of Econometrics* — Full article; skim the macroeconomics and industrial-organization sections

**Optional reading:** Gelman et al. (2014), *Beyond Power Calculations: Assessing Type S (Sign) and Type M (Magnitude) Errors*; Collaboration (2015), *Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science*; Deaton et al. (2018), *Understanding and misunderstanding randomized controlled trials*; Angrist et al. (2014), *Mastering ’Metrics: The Path from Cause to Effect*; Ioannidis (2005), *Why Most Published Research Findings Are False*

**Exercise:** Evidence Lab (simulation engine, 45 min) · **Case:** The Executive Disagreement (healthcare) · **Quiz:** 12 questions, 9 types · **Assignment:** Critique a Published Study · **Review cards:** 16 · **Glossary:** 20 terms

## Week 5 — Leadership Is a Social System

*Communication, Trust, Coaching & Executive Presence* · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 7 hours

> If leadership is partly something followers and observers construct, what can a leader actually do to earn trust, develop people and be understood?

**Objectives**

- Evaluate claims about leadership styles using the meta-analytic evidence and its measurement critique.
- Diagnose trust problems with the ability–benevolence–integrity model and distinguish psychological safety from comfort.
- Design feedback, delegation and accountability so that they keep attention on the task and make expectations explicit.
- Conduct a difficult conversation that holds a clear standard while managing defensiveness.
- Write one recommendation as an analytical memo, a one-page brief and a 60-second verbal explanation, each fit for its audience.

**Lessons**

1. **What Leadership Research Can and Cannot Tell You** (40 min) — Leadership research offers real but modest findings, and much of what passes for leadership knowledge is attribution after the fact.
2. **Trust and Psychological Safety: The Conditions for Hearing the Truth** (40 min) — Trust is a willingness to be vulnerable built on perceived ability, benevolence and integrity; psychological safety is a team’s shared belief that speaking up will not be punished — and neither is the same as comfort.
3. **Coaching, Feedback and Managing Through Others** (50 min) — Feedback helps on average but often backfires when it targets the person rather than the task, and leading through others means designing delegation and accountability, not only delivering messages.
4. **Executive Communication and the Question of Presence** (40 min) — Senior audiences need the recommendation first, the reasoning they will test, and the decision they must make; “executive presence” is better understood as other people’s inferences about your judgment and trustworthiness than as a trait.

**Required reading:** Meindl et al. (1985), *The Romance of Leadership* — Full article; read the introduction and the discussion; skim the individual studies.; Mayer et al. (1995), *An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust* — The definition of trust and the sections on ability, benevolence and integrity; skim the rest.; Edmondson (1999), *Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams* — Introduction, construct definition and discussion; skim the methods and results tables.

**Optional reading:** Knippenberg et al. (2013), *A Critical Assessment of Charismatic—Transformational Leadership Research: Back to the Drawing Board?*; Judge et al. (2004), *Transformational and Transactional Leadership: A Meta-Analytic Test of Their Relative Validity*; Kluger et al. (1996), *The Effects of Feedback Interventions on Performance: A Historical Review, a Meta-Analysis, and a Preliminary Feedback Intervention Theory*; Frazier et al. (2017), *Psychological Safety: A Meta-Analytic Review and Extension*; Jones et al. (2016), *The Effectiveness of Workplace Coaching: A Meta-Analysis of Learning and Performance Outcomes from Coaching*

## Week 6 — Power, Politics & Organizational Influence

*The Real Architecture of Organizations* · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 6.9 hours

> If the organization chart does not show who can make change happen, what does — and how should a leader use that knowledge without corroding the trust the organization runs on?

**Objectives**

- Analyze where power sits in an organization using the bases of power and the power-dependence relation, distinguishing formal authority from practical power.
- Map informal advice, trust and communication networks and identify brokers, single bridges and hidden power holders.
- Explain organizational change as the renegotiation of a political coalition and evaluate co-optation and legitimacy strategies, including their costs.
- Select influence tactics for upward, lateral and downward influence based on the evidence, and judge persuasion research by its replication record.
- Design a stakeholder engagement plan that sequences conversations, sets concessions and states explicit ethical lines.

**Lessons**

1. **Where Power Comes From: Bases, Dependence and the Chart That Lies** (40 min) — Power is a relation of dependence, not a line on an organization chart, and you can map where it actually sits before you try to change anything.
2. **The Organization Behind the Chart: Networks, Brokerage and Social Capital** (40 min) — Information, advice and trust move through informal networks whose structure — weak ties, bridges and structural holes — predicts who hears what first and whose ideas travel.
3. **Coalitions, Legitimacy and the Politics of Change** (40 min) — Organizations are negotiated settlements among coalitions embedded in fields that reward legitimacy; change succeeds when it renegotiates the settlement and earns legitimacy without being captured by it.
4. **Influence Without Authority: Tactics, Issue Selling and the Ethics of Persuasion** (35 min) — How you try to influence — upward, sideways or down — predicts whether people commit, comply or resist, and persuasion research is useful only when read with its replication record and an ethical line.

(1983), *The Iron Cage Revisited: Institutional Isomorphism and Collective Rationality in Organizational Fields* — Full article; focus on the three mechanisms of isomorphism and the hypotheses

**Optional reading:** Yukl et al. (1992), *Consequences of Influence Tactics Used with Subordinates, Peers, and the Boss*; Dutton et al. (1993), *Selling Issues to Top Management*; Rajkumar et al. ## Week 7 — Negotiation & Bargaining

*Interests, Leverage, Conflict & Agreements* · School of Law & Strategy · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 7 hours

> When two parties need each other but want different things, what determines whether they reach an agreement, how much value it creates, and who captures that value?

**Objectives**

- Prepare a negotiation by estimating both sides’ BATNAs, reservation values and aspirations and the resulting zone of possible agreement.
- Identify distributive, integrative and compatible issues and construct trades and contingent terms that move an agreement toward the Pareto frontier.
- Apply the evidence on anchoring, fixed-pie perception, fairness and situational moderators to decisions about first offers and defenses, stating that evidence’s limits.
- Design mandates, incentives and processes for negotiations conducted through agents, with internal constituencies, among several parties or across cultures.
- Distinguish legitimate competitive tactics from deception, including paltering, and distinguish estimates from commitments.
- Write a negotiation strategy memo that a decision-maker could approve and a negotiator could execute.

**Lessons**

1. **The Architecture of a Negotiation: Alternatives, Reservation Values and the Zone of Agreement** (37 min) — Before anyone speaks at the table, each side’s alternative, reservation value, aspiration and opening determine whether a deal is possible and roughly where it can land.
2. **Creating and Claiming Value: Integrative Bargaining, Logrolling and Contingent Contracts** (35 min) — Valuable deals are built from differences in priorities, forecasts, risk tolerance and timing, but the moves that create value also expose you to the moves that claim it.
3. **The Psychology of Bargaining: Anchors, Fairness, Emotion and Context** (32 min) — Real negotiators are pulled by first numbers, misread the other side’s interests, reject offers they see as unfair even at a cost, and perform differently depending on the situation — patterns you can anticipate and design around.
4. **Negotiating Through Others and Across Borders: Agents, Constituencies, Coalitions and Culture** (35 min) — Executive negotiations are usually conducted by agents, must be ratified by people who are not at the table, often involve more than two parties and frequently cross cultural lines — each of which changes the analysis.
5. **Where Hard Bargaining Ends: Deception, Commitments and Difficult Conversations** (32 min) — Negotiation rewards selective disclosure, which makes the boundary between tough bargaining and deception the most consequential line an executive draws — ethically, legally and for reputation.

**Required reading:** Lax et al. (1986), *The Manager as Negotiator: Bargaining for Cooperation and Competitive Gain* — Chapter 2, “The Negotiator’s Dilemma: Creating and Claiming Value” (pp. 29–45); Galinsky et al. (2001), *First offers as anchors: The role of perspective-taking and negotiator focus* — Full article; read the introduction, Experiment 1 and the general discussion closely, and skim Experiments 2–3; OpenCourseWare et al. (2001), *Negotiation and Conflict Management (15.667, Spring 2001)* — Syllabus and calendar, plus the study-materials handouts on negotiation styles and on bargaining tactics

**Optional reading:** Fisher et al. (1981), *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*; Thompson et al. (1990), *Social perception in negotiation*; Mazei et al. (2015), *A meta-analysis on gender differences in negotiation outcomes and their moderators*; Adair et al. (2001), *Negotiation behavior when cultures collide: The United States and Japan*; Rogers et al. (2017), *Artful paltering: The risks and rewards of using truthful statements to mislead others*

## Week 8 — Building Effective Organizations

*Organizational Design, Incentives & Management Systems* · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 7 hours

> How should an organization divide work, assign decisions and measure performance so that capable people accomplish more together than they could alone?

**Objectives**

- Explain how differentiation, integration and information-processing load determine which structure fits a task and its environment.
- Allocate decision rights by locating specific knowledge and weighing agency costs, and record them with a labelled practitioner tool.
- Evaluate the evidence that management practices cause performance, distinguishing correlational, experimental and long-run findings.
- Diagnose metric gaming with Goodhart’s law and the multitask incentive problem, and design countermeasures within an operating rhythm.
- Design a department—roles, spans, decision rights, coordination mechanisms, operating rhythm and metrics—and defend the trade-offs it accepts.

**Lessons**

1. **Why Structure Exists: Differentiation, Integration and Information Load** (36 min) — Structure is a bet about where information and coordination problems will arise; good designs match differentiation, integration, spans and layers to the uncertainty of the work.
2. **Who Decides? Decision Rights, Knowledge and Coordination** (36 min) — Decision rights should sit where the relevant knowledge is cheapest to find, but every move toward the knowledge raises agency costs, so rights, measures and rewards must be designed as one system.
3. **Does Management Matter? Practices, Measures and Their Limits** (38 min) — Structured management practices—monitoring, targets and people management—predict productivity and, in a randomized experiment, raised it; but measures that become targets invite gaming, and practices decay unless people and routines sustain them.
4. **Routines, Culture and Teams: The Organization Beneath the Chart** (34 min) — Organizations remember through routines, which make them reliable and also resistant to change; teams perform best when leaders design the conditions—a real team, direction, structure, context and coaching—rather than rely on personal style.

(1992), *Specific and General Knowledge, and Organizational Structure* — The sections on specific and general knowledge and on assigning decision rights (open SSRN version); Bloom et al. (2013), *Does Management Matter? Evidence from India* — Introduction and conclusion; skim the experimental design (the open NBER working-paper version reports an earlier, smaller first-year estimate)

**Optional reading:** Mintzberg (1980), *Structure in 5's: A Synthesis of the Research on Organization Design*; Holmström et al. (1991), *Multitask Principal–Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design*; Colfer et al. (2016), *The Mirroring Hypothesis: Theory, Evidence, and Exceptions*; Bloom et al. (2020), *Do Management Interventions Last? Evidence from India*; Hackman (2002), *Leading Teams: Setting the Stage for Great Performances*

## Week 9 — Business Strategy & Competitive Advantage

*Thinking Like a General Manager and Chief Strategy Officer* · School of Business & Leadership · 7 hours

> What makes an organization’s advantage defensible, and how should scarce resources be committed when rivals adapt and the future is uncertain?

**Objectives**

- Distinguish strategy from operational effectiveness and from goals, and rewrite an aspiration as a strategy kernel.
- Analyze how industry structure and added value determine who captures the value an organization creates.
- Evaluate resources and capabilities with the VRIN criteria, including the tautology critique, and apply them to build-versus-buy decisions.
- Explain why information-based advantages erode, using evidence from a professional sports labor market, and identify advantages that erode more slowly.
- Allocate scarce resources coherently under a chosen strategy and across scenarios, and diagnose peanut-butter allocation.

**Lessons**

1. **What Strategy Is — and What It Is Not** (38 min) — Strategy is a coherent set of choices about how to create distinctive value, which means accepting trade-offs and deciding what not to do; it is neither operational excellence nor a list of goals.
2. **Industry Structure and Value Capture** (38 min) — Creating value and capturing it are different problems: industry structure shapes how value is divided, and a firm can keep only what it adds that others cannot.
3. **Resources, Capabilities and the Build-versus-Buy Decision** (40 min) — Advantage that lasts usually rests on resources and capabilities rivals cannot buy or copy easily; the theory that says so is hard to use before the fact, and the build-versus-buy decision is where it becomes practical.
4. **Advantage Under Competition and Uncertainty** (40 min) — Edges built on information get competed away; durable strategy under uncertainty combines a few hard-to-reverse commitments with staged options, and is revealed by what the organization actually funds.

**Required reading:** Porter (1996), *What Is Strategy?* — Full article; Hakes et al. (2006), *An Economic Evaluation of the Moneyball Hypothesis* — Full article (14 pages); the regression tables can be skimmed

**Optional reading:** Rumelt (2011), *Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters*; Porter (2008), *The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy*; Brandenburger et al. (1996), *Value-based Business Strategy*; Barney (1991), *Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage*; Mintzberg et al. (1985), *Of Strategies, Deliberate and Emergent*

## Week 10 — Finance for Executive Decision-Making

*Understanding Money, Value & Resource Allocation* · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 7 hours

> How should a leader decide where scarce money and talent go when every benefit is uncertain and every yes forecloses something else?

**Objectives**

- Read and link the three financial statements, and diagnose gaps between accrual profit and operating cash flow.
- Compute and interpret NPV, IRR, payback and a weighted average cost of capital, and choose the right rule for mutually exclusive and capital-rationed decisions.
- Separate relevant from sunk and allocated costs, price opportunity cost explicitly, and reason at the margin.
- Evaluate investments under uncertainty with sensitivity and scenario analysis, real-options reasoning and reference-class forecasting.
- Value talent spending as surplus over cost against an explicit objective, and judge evidence about mispricing in talent markets.
- Write a financial justification that a skeptical decision-maker can audit.

**Lessons**

1. **Three Statements, One Story: Profit, Cash and Position** (30 min) — How the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement are built on accrual accounting, how they link, and why profit and cash can diverge sharply.
2. **The Price of Time: NPV, IRR, Payback and the Cost of Capital** (30 min) — Discounting turns cash flows at different dates into comparable values; NPV is the decision rule, IRR and payback are summaries with known traps, and the discount rate is an opportunity cost.
3. **Thinking at the Margin: Cost Behavior, Unit Economics and Capital Rationing** (30 min) — Decisions turn on incremental, future, avoidable costs and benefits — not averages, allocations or sunk costs — and when money is scarce, on value per constrained dollar.
4. **Valuation Under Uncertainty: Scenarios, Options and the Outside View** (35 min) — A single forecast is a guess; scenario and sensitivity analysis expose what drives value, real options price flexibility, and reference-class forecasting corrects the optimism built into base cases.
5. **Paying for Wins: The Economics of Talent Spending** (30 min) — Talent spending is capital allocation under uncertainty: value it as surplus over cost, at the margin, against an explicit objective — and expect any pricing edge to erode.

**Required reading:** Franklin et al. (2019), *Principles of Accounting, Volume 1: Financial Accounting* — Chapter 2, “Introduction to Financial Statements” — the sections on the four statements and how they connect (skip the end-of-chapter exercises unless the material is new).; Dahlquist et al. ## Week 11 — Law for Business Leaders

*Legal Reasoning, Contracts & Institutional Constraints* · School of Law & Strategy · School of Business & Leadership · 7 hours

> How can a leader who is not a lawyer recognize legal issues early, read agreements as allocations of risk, and know which questions belong to counsel?

**Objectives**

- Apply an issue–rule–application–conclusion structure, with attention to burden of proof and jurisdiction, to business fact patterns.
- Analyze formation, interpretation, conditions, representations, warranties and remedies in commercial agreements.
- Evaluate employment terms — at-will status, restrictive covenants, confidentiality and invention assignment — against the current U.S. legal landscape and the empirical evidence.
- Explain agency, fiduciary duties and the business judgment rule, and recognize institutional constraints such as regulation, labor law and league rules.
- Identify, rank and explain legal and commercial issues in a fictional agreement, and frame precise questions and plain-language redlines for qualified counsel.
- Judge when and how to involve counsel, including the basics of privilege and preservation.

**Lessons**

1. **Legal Reasoning for Non-Lawyers: Prediction, Rules and Proof** (35 min) — Legal reasoning is disciplined prediction: name the issue, find the governing rule, apply it to the facts, and estimate how a decision-maker would rule and with what confidence.
2. **Contracts: Formation, Meaning, Risk and Remedies** (35 min) — A contract is a set of enforceable promises whose value lies less in the threat of a lawsuit than in how clearly it allocates obligations, conditions and risks before anything goes wrong.
3. **Employment Agreements, Restrictive Covenants and Who Owns the Work** (35 min) — Employment terms that look like boilerplate — at-will status, confidentiality, invention assignment and restrictive covenants — decide who may leave, where they may go and who owns what they create.
4. **Agency, Fiduciary Duty and Institutional Constraints** (30 min) — People who act for others — agents, officers, directors — owe duties of care and loyalty, and every organization operates inside institutional constraints that limit what any single agreement can do.
5. **Working With Counsel: Timing, Privilege and Legal Risk** (30 min) — Using lawyers well means involving them at the right moment, protecting privileged communications, asking questions that produce forecasts, and owning the business judgment that remains.

**Required reading:** Valbrune et al. (2019), *Business Law I Essentials* — Selected sections on contracts (formation, consideration, remedies), agency and employment law — choose the matching topics from the book’s table of contents.; Jr. (1897), *The Path of the Law* — The first half of the essay, through the “bad man” passage and the definition of law as prediction.; Starr et al. **Optional reading:** Kaplow (1992), *Rules Versus Standards: An Economic Analysis*; Macaulay (1963), *Non-Contractual Relations in Business: A Preliminary Study*; Marx et al. ## Week 12 — Strategy into Execution

*Change Management, Operations & Organizational Implementation* · School of Business & Leadership · School of Behavioral & Social Science · 7 hours

> Why do sound strategies so often fail to change what an organization actually does, and how should a leader sequence change so that it takes hold?

**Objectives**

- Diagnose a stalled initiative using evidence about resource allocation, cross-unit coordination and improvement time rather than stated priorities.
- Evaluate popular change models and statistics, distinguishing practitioner heuristics from tested findings.
- Assess readiness for change as commitment and efficacy, and match interventions to each.
- Apply Little’s law, bottleneck logic and leading and lagging indicators to design an operating rhythm for a change.
- Design a multi-site rollout that separates core components from adaptable periphery and accounts for institutional differences.
- Produce a 90-day implementation roadmap with phases, decision points, stakeholders, metrics and risks.

**Lessons**

1. **Why Execution Fails: The Gap Between Choosing and Doing** (40 min) — Strategies stall less because people disobey than because coordination, reallocation and improvement time are starved by the daily operation.
2. **Change Models and the Evidence Behind Them** (40 min) — The most popular change models are useful practitioner checklists with thin evidence, while readiness—commitment plus efficacy—gives you something you can actually diagnose.
3. **Operations: Flow, Bottlenecks, Variability and Standard Work** (40 min) — A small set of operations ideas—Little’s law, bottlenecks, the cost of high utilization, and standard work as experiment—explains why most implementations clog and how to run them.
4. **Implementation Across Cultures and Institutions** (40 min) — Moving a practice across sites and countries succeeds when you hold the core constant, adapt the periphery, and diagnose institutions and context rather than relying on national stereotypes.

## Week 14 — The Executive Capstone

*Integrating Human Behavior, Strategy, Law & Leadership* · School of Behavioral & Social Science · School of Business & Leadership · School of Law & Strategy · 7 hours

> How does a leader combine behavior, evidence, strategy, money, law and ethics into one decision that can be built, presented, defended under questioning and learned from afterward?

**Objectives**

- Integrate behavioral, evidentiary, strategic, financial, legal and ethical considerations into a single recommendation, and identify which consideration is binding.
- Explain the liability of newness and the competing liability-of-adolescence pattern, and use them to sequence the first year of a new function.
- Structure, present and defend a major decision for a skeptical senior audience, including a premortem, a steelmanned alternative and direct answers to hard questions.
- Distinguish decision quality from outcome quality, and design an after-action review that produces learning rather than blame.
- Design a personal evidence practice — journal, forecasts, scheduled reviews and a learning agenda — sized to the small number of major decisions an executive makes.

**Lessons**

1. **Integrative Judgment: One Decision, Six Lenses** (40 min) — Major decisions rarely fail for lack of analysis inside one discipline; they fail where disciplines meet, and this lesson gives you a disciplined way to integrate them.
2. **Building a Function from Zero: Newness, Legitimacy and the First Ninety Days** (40 min) — New units are fragile for predictable, structural reasons, so the first months should be sequenced to build legitimacy and durable decision rights before the initial mandate runs out.
3. **Presenting and Defending Decisions to Senior Leaders and Boards** (40 min) — A decision presentation is an argument made under questioning: structure it around the decision, stress-test it with a premortem and a steelmanned alternative, and answer hard questions directly.
4. **The Leader’s Learning Agenda: Outcomes, Process and After-Action Reviews** (35 min) — Executives make too few big decisions to learn from outcomes alone, so they learn by judging process separately from results, running honest after-action reviews and keeping a personal evidence practice.

(1988), *Outcome Bias in Decision Evaluation* — Introduction, the first experiment and the general discussion.
