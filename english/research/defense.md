# Defensive instruction in English: research summary

For CP English lessons that teach Spanish-speaking coaches in Dominican academies to give defensive instruction in natural American English. Compiled November 2026.

**How this was sourced.** Web search was available while this was compiled, but full-page fetches were blocked by the network sandbox (mlb.com, littleleague.org, baseball-reference.com and acento.com.do all failed to resolve). Each sourced claim below was checked against the text that search engines returned from the cited page. It was **not** checked against the full page. Claims marked **[judgement]** are the compiler's working knowledge of American baseball coaching speech. They are not backed by a source here, and a coach or editor should review them before they become lesson text. Bracketed numbers point to the bibliography in §5.

## 1. Core defensive fundamentals and their coaching language

Coaching language varies by program. The phrases below are common, but none is a single "official" wording.

### Ready position and pre-pitch setup
- **Meaning:** The athletic stance a fielder is in as the ball enters the hitting zone: feet wider than the shoulders, knees bent, weight on the balls of the feet and evenly spread, hands and glove out in front. TeamSnap lists three points: crouch, stay on the balls of the feet, and keep weight evenly distributed as the pitch is delivered [6]. The Cal Ripken manual lets players creep or move side to side, but they "must be in ready position as ball enters hitting zone" [5].
- **Coaches say:** "Get ready." "Be ready on every pitch." "Get down." "Weight forward." "On your toes" or "on the balls of your feet." "Hands out front." **[judgement on the short forms]**

### Creep / prep step
- **Meaning:** Small walking steps toward the plate as the pitcher delivers, ending with a timed hop or step (a "ready step" or "prep step") that lands as the ball reaches the hitting zone. The Ripken manual describes the ready step as "left step, right step, left step, and present your glove" for a right-hander, reversed for a left-hander [5].
- **Coaches say:** "Creep in." "Time your prep step." "Land when the ball gets to the zone." **[judgement on phrasing]**

### First step and reads
- **Meaning:** The first move after the ball is hit. It needs to be quick and in the right direction. Infielders read the hop. Outfielders read the ball's flight: in, back, or to the side.
- **Coaches say:** "Good first step." "First step was late." "Read it off the bat." "Read the hop." **[judgement]**

### Fielding a ground ball
- **Approach and angle.** Come get the ball and build momentum toward the target. Don't circle it. Dugout Captain: "keep it simple… come get the baseball" [8]. One infield handout compares a "V" cut with "rounding the ball off". It says the cut lets the fielder take the ball a hop earlier and closer to the target [9]. That handout's author is not identified. Coaches say: "Charge the ball." "Come get it." "Take a good angle." "Don't drift." "Don't let the ball play you." **[judgement on the last three]**
- **Work through the ball.** Keep moving through the catch and toward the throw, instead of stopping on the ball. One team's practice notes call "Work Through The Ball" a common cue. They warn that the body can get ahead of the hands when momentum carries toward first [10]. No authoritative definition was found.
- **Glove out front.** Field the ball out in front of the body, where you can see it. Dugout Captain's cue is "hands out in front (alligator mouth)" [8]. Coaches say: "Glove out front." "Get the glove down early." "See it into the glove."
- **Where on the body.** Many coaches teach fielding the ball in the middle of the body, or slightly to the glove side (for a right-hander, slightly left of center). **[judgement: programs differ on this point]**
- **Funnel.** After the catch, bring the ball and glove up into the belly or belt area with soft hands. This starts the transfer. Little League's progression stresses that "a focus must be placed not only on the hands, but also more importantly, on the feet" [7]. Coaches say: "Soft hands." "Funnel it in." "Bring it to your belly."
- **Stay down.** Keep the glove and body low until the ball is in the glove. Don't lift your head early. Coaches say: "Stay down." "Stay low." "Don't come up early." **[judgement]**

### Backhand and forehand
- **Backhand:** Fielding a ball on the throwing-hand side by reaching across the body with the glove turned over. This is the shortstop's play in the hole. MLB en español describes such a play as fielding "con el guante de revés en el hueco del campo corto" [29].
- **Forehand:** Fielding a ball on the glove side with the glove open, palm toward the ball.
- **Coaches say:** "Backhand it." "Take it on the backhand." "Forehand, open glove." "Don't round it, backhand it." **[judgement]**

### Transfer, footwork and setting the feet
- **Transfer:** Moving the ball from the glove to the throwing hand quickly and cleanly, with a four-seam grip if there is time. Coaches say: "Quick transfer." "Get it out of the glove." "Find the seams."
- **Footwork / "feet to throw":** After the catch, move the feet into a line toward the target before throwing, using a shuffle or crow hop. The infield handout calls this throwing sequence "Down-Out-Up-In" [9]. Coaches say: "Use your feet." "Feet to throw." "Set your feet." "Shuffle and throw." "Line up your feet to the target." **[judgement on most of these]**
- **Throwing lane:** A clear line from the thrower to the receiver, with no runner or fielder in the way. The thrower may have to step off that line, or drop the arm angle. Coaches say: "Clear your lane." "Get out of the runner's line." **[judgement]**

### Double-play feeds and pivots (brief)
Middle infielders feed the ball to the man covering second (flip, toss, or throw), and the pivot man touches the bag and throws to first. Coaches say: "Give him a good feed." "Show him the ball." "Feed it chest high." "Turn two." "Get the lead runner." **[judgement]** In Spanish: *doble play* or *doble matanza* [34].

### Outfield: drop step, reads and routes
- **Drop step:** On a ball hit over the outfielder's head, the first step goes back with the foot on the ball's side and the hips open, then the fielder runs (crossover and go). Several coaching sources treat a backward first step as the default and warn against backpedaling [11]. Coaches say: "Drop step and go." "Open your hips." "Don't backpedal." "First step back."
- **Route:** The path an outfielder runs to the ball. A good route is efficient and gets the fielder behind the ball, moving toward the throw. Coaches say: "Take a good route." "Run to the spot." "Don't drift." "Bad route there, you banana'd it." **[judgement]** ("Banana route" is a common American term for a curved route. It is listed here as judgement only.)
- **Get behind the ball / crow hop:** On a ball the fielder has to throw, they catch it while moving toward the target, then crow hop: a hop and step that turns momentum into the throw. One drill source describes the crow hop as stepping forward with the back foot to build momentum toward the target (search excerpt; source not reliably attributable). Coaches say: "Get behind it." "Catch it coming in." "Crow hop and throw."
- **Hit the cutoff:** Throw through the cutoff man, not to him. The ABCA's *Inside Pitch* recommends "one long hop" to the base [12]. MLB.com defines the cutoff man as "a fielder who takes the incoming throw from an outfielder after a hit" [4]. Cutoff and relay are different jobs. The cutoff man can redirect or stop a throw that would otherwise go all the way to a base. The relay man is the middle link on a long throw from deep in the outfield. Coaches say: "Hit the cutoff." "Keep it low." "Hit him in the chest." **[judgement on the last two]**

### Communication calls
- Common calls for a fly or pop-up: **"I got it"**, **"Ball, ball"**, **"Mine, mine"** (coaching sources compiled via search [13][14]). Many systems have the player call it three times and loud, and only once they're sure they can reach the ball [13].
- **Priority:** The center fielder has priority over the corner outfielders, and outfielders have priority over infielders [14]. Programs differ on the details.
- **"You, you" / "Take it"** tells a teammate to make the play. No source in this review confirmed "you, you" as standard. **[judgement: common, but treat it as a team convention]**
- **Throw directions** shouted by the cutoff or by the catcher: "Cut!" (catch it), "Cut two!" (catch it and throw to second), "Relay!", "Four!" or "Home!", "Let it go!" (don't touch it) **[judgement: standard wording varies by team]**. **"Two!"** before the pitch means a double play is on, and in a play it means "throw to second."

### Positioning
- **Shade:** Move a few steps toward one side ("shade him to pull", "shade up the middle"). **Depth:** How deep the fielder plays ("in", "double-play depth", "back", "normal depth").
- **Pull / oppo:** A hitter who "pulls" hits to his own side of the field. "Oppo" (opposite field) is the other side. Coaches say: "He's a dead pull hitter, cheat over." "Play him oppo." **[judgement]**
- **Shift limits (MLB, since 2023):** When the pitch is released, at least four players must have both feet on the infield dirt, with two infielders entirely on each side of second base. MLB.com's Statcast glossary says a full shift (three infielders on one side of second) "is no longer allowed" [1][2]. Infielders may still shade within their side. Minor-league and academy play generally follow the same rule, but check the league in question **[unverified for the DSL]**.

### "Error"
MLB.com defines an error as a play on which a fielder "fails to convert an out on a play that an average fielder should have made" [3]. See §3 for how it is phrased.

## 2. How American coaches give defensive instruction

### Style (observed practice, **[judgement]** unless cited)
- **Short imperatives, one cue at a time.** "Stay down." "Glove out front." "Feet." Coaches stack cues across reps, not inside one sentence.
- **Demonstrate, then rep.** "Watch me." "Like this." "Now you." "Your turn."
- **Explain the why, briefly.** "Get your feet under you, so your throw has something behind it." "Catch it coming in. It saves you a step on the throw."
- **Check understanding.** "Does that make sense?" "You with me?" "Show me." "What did you feel there?" "Where was your glove?" Asking the player to show or describe is a better check than "Do you understand?", which nearly always gets a yes.
- **Positive and specific feedback.** "Good, that's it, glove out front." "Better. Your first step was quicker." "Nice job staying down." Correction comes in the same breath: "Good effort. Now stay down through it."
- **Running a drill.** "Two lines." "Let's go." "Ball in, ball out." "Reset." "Good, again." "Let's do three more." "Last one." "Bring it in." (gather). "Water." "Let's get one more good one."

### Principles from motor-learning research (stated cautiously)
- **External focus of attention.** Wulf's 2013 review of 15 years of studies found that instructions focusing on the movement's effect (an external focus, e.g. "throw it through his chest", "get the glove to the ball") generally improved performance and learning compared with instructions focusing on body parts (an internal focus, e.g. "bend your elbow") [15]. A 2021 meta-analysis reported advantages for external focus, larger for retention and transfer than for immediate performance [16]. Dissent exists: a 2013 systematic review called the preference "not sufficiently substantiated" [21]. *For lessons:* teach both kinds of cue, and show external cues as a useful option, not as a rule.
- **Feedback frequency.** The guidance hypothesis (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984) proposes that feedback after every attempt can make learners dependent on it [19]. Empirical support is mixed. Wulf et al. (2010) found that frequent feedback was *better* when it was external-focus [20]. *For lessons:* teach coaches the language to give feedback and to hold it back ("You tell me how that one felt").
- **Expectancies and autonomy.** Wulf and Lewthwaite's OPTIMAL theory (2016) argues that enhanced expectancies (positive framing) and learner autonomy (choices) help learning [17]. A 2023 meta-analysis argues that publication bias and underpowered designs have substantially exaggerated these motivational benefits [18]. *For lessons:* teach positive, specific language because it is good practice and normal in American clubhouses. Do not present it as proven to speed up skill learning.

## 3. What Spanish-speaking coaches find hard

Grammar transfer points have general support in learner-English literature. Baseball-specific examples are **[judgement]**.

1. **Possessives with body parts.** Spanish uses the definite article ("dobla las rodillas"). English instruction to a player normally uses *your*: "Bend your knees", "Get your glove down", "Move your feet". Swan & Smith's *Learner English* (Spanish chapter) documents this transfer, as cited in secondary teaching sources [22]. "Bend the knees" is not wrong, but it sounds like a manual rather than a coach. **[judgement]**
2. **Articles with bases and positions.** "Throw to first", "play short", "cover second", "at third". No article, and normally no "base". Learners may say "throw to the first base". **[judgement]**
3. **Error.** In general English, people *make* mistakes, not "commit" them. In baseball English, though, **"commit an error"** is standard ("He committed two errors"), and so is "make an error". The thing to fix is "commit a mistake". Spanish media use *cometer errores* [3]. **[judgement on usage frequency]**
4. **"Take / catch / get the ball."** *Coger la bola* covers catch, field, pick up and get. English separates them: *catch* (a fly or a throw), *field* (a ground ball), *pick up* (a ball on the ground), *get* or *get to* (reach it). "I got it" is the fixed call. **[judgement]**
5. **Phrasal verbs.** Spanish is verb-framed: the direction goes in the verb (*entrar, subir, retroceder*). English often uses verb + particle: *come in, back up, get behind, stay down, work through, line up, cut off, pick up, throw out*. Alejo González (2010) found that Spanish and Italian learners produced fewer *out*-phrasal verbs than learners whose first languages are satellite-framed [23]. Avoidance of phrasal verbs is documented for other learners whose first language lacks them [24]. *Teach these as chunks*, with the action shown.
6. **Two meanings of "back up".** "Back up second" means stand behind the base in case of an overthrow. "Back up!" means move backward. Context separates them. **[judgement]**
7. **False friends to watch.** *lanzar* (pitch, or any throw) vs English *pitch*, which is used only for the pitcher; *realizar* ≠ realize; *actualmente* ≠ actually; *asistir* (attend) vs *assist* (in fielding stats, a throw that leads to an out); *molestar* ≠ molest (a serious false friend); *discusión* (an argument) vs *discussion*. **[judgement; standard ELT lists]**
8. **Make vs do.** Spanish *hacer* covers both. "Make the play", "make the throw", "do your job", "do it again", "make an adjustment". [Learner-profile material, not peer-reviewed.] **[judgement]**
9. **Register.** Short direct imperatives ("Stay down", "Move your feet", "Again") are normal and not rude in American baseball. "Please" in every cue sounds odd on the field. Warmth comes from tone, the player's name, praise, and phrases like "Let's…", "Give me…", "Nice." Use softer forms off the field and with staff: "Can you…?", "Would you mind…?". **[judgement: no study of baseball clubhouse register was found]**
10. **Phrasing that works whatever the accent.** Keep cues to one to three words, repeat them the same way every time, pair each with a gesture, and use stressed content words ("FEET", "GLOVE DOWN"). Avoid cues that depend on hard sound contrasts, like *ship/sheep*, or *b/v*, which Spanish does not distinguish. **[judgement]**
