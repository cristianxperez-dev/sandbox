# Pitching: research summary for Spanish lessons

Research notes for lessons that teach English-speaking pitching coaches to work in Spanish with Spanish-speaking pitchers, mostly in Dominican Republic academies. Compiled November 13, 2026.

## 1. Grips

Main source: Driveline's *Ultimate Guide to Baseball Pitch Grips* (Chris Langin, updated April 22, 2022) [1], read in full.

**Driveline's approach**
- Driveline photographs and measures the grip of every athlete who comes through its facility. Grips are logged under a fixed tracking system, so that small variations can be compared and pitchers matched to grips faster [1].
- The guide's core message is that **"the grip is only as important as the movement profile it creates."** Learning a pitch means more than copying a famous grip: "Things may not click right away, so we should expect tweaks" [1].
- Driveline's question for each pitcher is "what is the best pitch grip per pitch type for this pitcher right now?" It balances the grip, the player's comfort and a specific release [1].

**The five factors Driveline tracks when adjusting a grip** [1]:

1. **Tilt and ball position.** Tilting a base grip inward or outward, or shifting the fingers toward the thumb side or the pinky side. This slightly changes seam orientation and how the fingers come off the ball.
2. **Fingertip pressure (coaching cues).** If a comfortable grip doesn't move well, "a simple grip pressure adjustment might do the trick." Cueing more pressure on the index or middle finger changes the pitch.
   - Cues change movement too. A pitcher told to "throw a sideways curveball" throws a different shape from one told to throw "as hard as humanly possible."
   - Example cues: "stay through the ball", "throw the back of your hand at the target", "throw it like a football".
   - Driveline: "We feel it's best to be flexible with cues and worry less about forcing one cue to work."
3. **Thumb position.** Logged as 0 / 25 / 50, and as "flat" or "tucked."
   - A thumb **underneath** the ball may make it easier to "stay through the ball" at release.
   - Moving the thumb **to the side** may make it easier to "get around the ball."
4. **Spike alterations.** Curling, spiking, lifting off ("index off") or stacking the index finger.
   - Spiking can put more middle-fingertip pressure on the seam without a coach having to cue it.
   - Adding a spike may give a curveball more drop. Taking the spike off may move spin direction upward, which can help a slider.
5. **Depth of grip.** Grip depth is "a big part of grip comfort" and depends partly on hand size.
   - Some pitchers need to choke a four-seamer into the palm for feel; others leave space between palm and ball.
   - Changing depth can also help a pitcher find a seam to pull off, or get the thumb somewhere more comfortable.

**Pitch types and common grip variations.** Driveline groups pitches into six main types [1]:

| Pitch | What Driveline reports [1] |
|---|---|
| **Four-seam fastball** | Grips are "relatively standard." The two main variations are finger width (narrow or wide) and whether the seam sits under the **fingertip or the first knuckle**. Little tilt variation; thumb flat or tucked; no spike variations. Cues: "Stay behind the ball," "Pull the ball down," "Apply more pressure on the ball," "Hold the ball tighter," "Pronate sooner." |
| **Two-seam / sinker** | Close and wide two-seam grips. "Seams and tilt can be important": because of seam-shifted wake, "minor adjustments on the seam and slight cut can cause two seamers to move more than expected." Cues: "Stay through it," "Get over the top of it," "Roll over the ball." |
| **Cutter** | Often a four-seam grip **tilted outward** "to get on the side of the ball while still feeling like a fastball." Another version uses a two-seam-like grip with a different release. Cues: "focus on middle finger pressure," "Think palm facing first base," "Think slider." Cues depend on the starting point: turning a four-seamer into a cutter is different from taking movement off a slider. Moving the thumb further under the ball can help turn a slider into a cutter. |
| **Slider** | Middle-finger pressure helps the pitcher get around the ball. Index-finger pressure may shift spin direction upward and move a slurve toward a slider. Moving the thumb more to the side may help turn a cutter into a slider. Cues: "Throw the back of the hand," "Throw it like a football," "Pull on the side of the ball," "Slash the zone in half diagonally." |
| **Curveball** | Cues: "Get in front of the ball," "Spin the ball with your middle finger," "Throw with the back of your hand." Spiking the index finger may help put more pressure on the middle finger without a cue. |
| **Changeup** | A pitcher with a two-seam grip may tilt the fingers toward the thumb "to get away from a seam at release and decrease raw spin." Some "choke" the ball deep to lessen spin and velocity; others grip it in the fingertips "like an egg." Cues: "Roll over the ball," "Throw it with your ring finger," "Pronate sooner," "Swipe inside of the ball," "Think about having a flexible wrist." |

**Additions from other sources:**
- The four-seam "horseshoe" grip in Spanish press: *"los dedos índice y medio del pitcher quedan planos rodeando la 'herradura' de la pelota"* [33].
- Splitters spin very little. Justin Martínez's split-fingered pitch averaged about 630 rpm, "un poco menos de la mitad" of an average MLB splitter [31]. MLB.com says lower spin on changeups and splitters tends to give more downward "tumbling" movement [6].
- A circle changeup with the thumb and index finger forming a circle is described in Spanish in [49]. The Driveline guide doesn't name the circle change specifically.

**Velocity and intensity matter when testing grips** [1]:
- Bullpens and pitch-design sessions are often thrown below game intensity: "Some pitch design sessions are thrown at 70% RPE, others at 95% RPE."
- Breaking balls "will always play better in the 95% sessions." Driveline judges a pitch's shape relative to that day's fastball velocity.
- In MLB data, a 1 mph fastball gain goes with about 0.75 mph more slider velocity.

**Measuring the change.**
- Driveline takes a "baseline" profile with Rapsodo or TrackMan when an athlete arrives, then tests cues and grips against it.
- Logging what has been tried lets coaches rule out shapes and "easily recall optimal grip/cue combinations" when the pitcher later loses feel [1].

**What is individual.**
- Hand size, comfort, arm slot and release decide what a grip produces. Driveline treats every grip as something to test and tweak, not copy [1].
- MLB.com en español's piece on an unusual grip shows how much grips vary [32].

## 2. Pitch design

**Spin rate.** Revolutions per minute (rpm) at release [6]. Savant's glossary: "How much spin, in revolutions per minute, a pitch was thrown with" [3]. MLB.com en español uses *tasa de rotación* [50].

**Spin axis and spin direction (the clock face).**
- The Magnus effect explains most pitch movement, and that movement is **perpendicular to the spin axis** [2].
- Driveline states the axis both as clock time and in degrees. Its example is a right-handed sinker "spinning at an axis just higher than 1:30 (233 degrees) at 100% efficiency," which should get roughly equal horizontal and vertical break (arm-side run and rise) [2].
- In the usual pitching convention, 12:00 is pure backspin (force straight up) and 6:00 is pure topspin (a "12–6" curveball). This comes from Rapsodo material via search [13]. The Savant pages [3] [4] don't define the clock face.
- Savant shows each pitcher's spin direction per pitch type "from the hitter's perspective," with the bar length proportional to how often the pitch spins in that direction. A Pitcher POV / Batter POV switch changes the view [3]. So coaches should say which viewpoint a reading uses.
- **Coaches say:** "Your fastball was at 1:15, let's see if we can get it closer to 12:30."

**Active spin / spin efficiency.**
- Savant's glossary: "Statcast refers to the spin that contributes to movement as Active Spin" [3] [7].
- Rapsodo's "spin efficiency" is true spin divided by total spin. Pure gyro spin, like a football spiral, is 0% efficient. Gyro degree runs from 0° (pure transverse) to 90° (pure gyro) [12].
- Higher efficiency is **not always better**: gyro sliders rely on low efficiency [12].
- Driveline notes that on breaking balls and other pitches below 100% efficiency, "some amount of gyrospin is converted to transverse spin during ball flight." That conversion "typically moves pitches to the glove side" [2].

**Induced vertical break (IVB) and horizontal break.** Savant's pitch-movement page [4] shows two views:
- **"Total Movement"** is real-world movement, including gravity. Because "gravity requires time, and slower pitches aren't 'better' just because they have more time to move," each pitch is compared with "other pitches of the same pitch type, within +/- 2 MPH and +/- 0.5 feet of extension/release."
- **"Induced Movement (or IVB)"** is "reported without gravity, and attempts to isolate movement created by the pitcher's ability to spin and manipulate the ball."
- The leaderboard columns include horizontal break, vertical drop and IVB, each in inches, plus "vs Comparable", "vs Avg" and "% Break vs Avg" versions [4].
- MLB.com glossary: positive IVB is "rise" (really less drop than expected) and negative is extra drop. 2024 MLB averages were about +16 in. for four-seamers and about −10 in. for curveballs [5] (search excerpt).

**Spin-based vs observed movement, and deviation.** These definitions come from Savant's spin-direction leaderboard [3]:
- Each pitcher gets two charts. **Observed movement** "is shown based on plate location and initial velocity vector." **Spin-based movement** "is shown over the initial part of the flight towards home."
- **Deviation:** "Difference in Direction between Observed Movement and Spin-Based Movement."
- Savant: "While the two charts will generally agree, slight differences between the movement of spin at pitch release and upon reaching home implies additional aerodynamic forces at work."

**Seam-shifted wake (SSW). A research concept, not a coaching cue.** Main source: Driveline's *More Than What It Seams* [2], read in full; it uses the 2020 season, the first year of Hawk-Eye tracking.

- **The problem it addresses.** Scouts describe "late" movement on great pitches. Physicists had called it an optical illusion: the pitch was moving exactly as its spin, release point and velocity predicted [2].
- **What it is.**
  - A smooth surface and a seamed or rough surface treat the airflow differently: turbulent flow "sticks to the ball longer" than smooth flow.
  - In Driveline's words: "when you break the symmetry of a wake, creating turbulence on one side, you can create force, and thus movement, that isn't typical. This can be achieved through seam orientation, hence a seam-shifted wake."
  - This force is separate from the Magnus force, which comes from spin [2].
  - Dr. Barton Smith and colleagues at Utah State University (USU) researched it and coined the term. Earlier, Alan Nathan had shown with PITCHf/x data that much of a 2011 Freddy Garcia splitter's acceleration "wasn't explained by Magnus force" [2] [14] [15].
- **Why sinkers.** Most SSW work had focused on arm-side pitches: sinkers, two-seamers and changeups. The effect "seems to be the most pronounced amongst sinkers." Sinkers also spin close to 100% efficiency, which isolates the SSW effect better than breaking balls do [2].
- **How Driveline measured it.**
  - It compared the spin axis **inferred from movement** with the spin axis Hawk-Eye **observed** at release, and called the difference "2D axis deviation."
  - A positive deviation means the pitch moves more to the arm side and/or down than its observed axis predicts, which implies SSW.
  - The 2020 league average for sinkers was about **17.6°**. Kyle Hendricks and Dallas Keuchel were among the leaders [2].
- **Grips.**
  - Of the top 20 pitchers by deviation, twelve were documented throwing a **one-seam sinker**: "one or both fingers living on only one seam of the baseball." That surprised Driveline, because the "Laminar Express" two-seamer had been "the poster child" [2].
  - One athlete's four-seamer and one-seamer had about 1° difference in measured axis at release. Their movement-inferred axes differed by nearly 30°, worth nearly 5 in. more run and 6 in. more drop than his four-seamer [2].
- **Does it matter?**
  - On the 2020 sample, sinkers with more deviation beat their expected run values.
  - Controlling for arm angle, velocity and movement, deviation still explained some decrease in runs. In variable importance it ranked ahead of release speed and horizontal break, just behind vertical break [2].
  - Driveline calls this an introductory, limited-sample result.
- **Driveline's plain-language takeaway:** "specific orientations of the baseball at release can give an athlete access to movement profiles that normally would only be achievable by shifting their arm slot or spin axis drastically" [2].
- **Since then:**
  - MLB.com reports the share of sinkers with seam-shifted movement rose from 38% (2020) to 49% (2025) [17] (search excerpt).
  - SSW is also discussed for changeups, such as Tarik Skubal's [18].
- **Lesson implication:** a coach would say "the seams are giving you extra sink," or would test seam orientation and measure the result. A coach would not give SSW as a cue.

**Release consistency and pitch shape.**
- "Shape" means a pitch's combination of velocity, spin and movement.
- **Repeatable release** means the same release point and arm angle every time [10]. It keeps shape consistent and makes different pitches look alike out of the hand **(unsourced: general coaching usage)**.
- **Typical changes coaches look for (unsourced: general coaching usage):**
  - more separation between pitches: velocity gap, and movement in different directions, such as a fastball with carry next to a changeup with fade and drop;
  - a breaking ball whose movement differs clearly from the fastball;
  - tighter grouping of the same pitch from throw to throw.
- "Desirable" depends on the pitcher's arm angle, fastball velocity that day [1] and whole arsenal, so no single target fits everyone.

**Communicating readouts.**
- **Edgertronic** high-speed video shows the hand and seams at release in slow motion; Driveline used Edgertronic footage to show a one-seamer next to a four-seamer [2]. **Rapsodo** gives real-time ball-flight numbers [23].
- Driveline's workflow: baseline profile, then cue/grip tests against it, then a log of what worked [1].
- A common workflow is throw, look at the readout, ask what he felt, then connect the feeling to the number [23] (search excerpt; vendor and team anecdotes).
- In practice, coaches turn numbers into **ball flight and feel**: "more ride", "it ran more", "it was firmer", "it spun more like a bullet". They show the movement plot or video. They do not read raw decimals aloud **(unsourced: general coaching usage)**.

## 3. Delivery and mechanics

Short working definitions follow. Most are standard coaching vocabulary (**unsourced: general coaching usage**) except where a citation is given. The ASMI-linked review by Fortenbaugh, Fleisig and Andrews (2009) [19] gathers the kinematic variables, upper and lower body, that relate to ball velocity. It also notes that higher velocity goes with higher loads on the elbow and shoulder. Only its abstract-level content was seen.

- **Setup.** Starting position on the rubber (windup or stretch/set), including feet, hands and how the ball is hidden.
- **Balance / posture.** Controlling the centre of mass over the back leg, with the trunk in a stable position, before moving toward the plate.
- **Leg lift.** Raising the stride leg. Height and tempo vary widely between pitchers.
- **Stride direction.** Where the lead foot lands relative to a line from the rubber to the plate: closed, on line or open.
- **Stride length.** How far the lead foot lands from the rubber, usually expressed as a percentage of the pitcher's height.
- **Pelvis and trunk rotation.** The hips turn toward the plate, then the trunk. Most pitchers start turning the hips before foot strike. This and the next item come from secondary summaries citing Fleisig and colleagues, not the papers themselves.
- **Hip–shoulder separation.** The angle between pelvis and shoulders in the horizontal plane around foot strike. One study linked it to trunk rotation velocity and, through that, to pitch velocity. Commercial sources quote "ideal" ranges, but separation depends on the athlete's body, so **no single number is correct**.
- **Kinematic sequence / timing.** Segments peak in order: pelvis, trunk, arm, hand. Energy moves "up the chain" [19] (secondary summary).
- **Arm path.** The route the throwing arm takes from break of hands to release (short or long, high or low).
- **Arm angle / arm slot.** Statcast measures arm angle at release: 0° is a sidearm (horizontal) and 90° is straight over the top. Data go back to 2020 [10]. MLB.com en español reported Jacob deGrom's 2025 average at 30°, his lowest in Statcast's five years of the measure [30].
- **Release point.** Where in space the ball leaves the hand (height, side, distance from the rubber).
- **Extension.** How far in front of the rubber the pitcher releases the ball, in feet. More extension can make a 93 mph fastball "look like" 96 mph, and it feeds "perceived velocity" [8].
- **Lead-leg block.** The front leg firming up (stopping and often straightening) after foot strike, so rotation is redirected up the body.
- **Follow-through.** Deceleration of the arm and body after release.
- **Consistency / repeatability.** Doing the same delivery and release pitch to pitch.

**Safety and individualization.**
- Mechanics are individual. Strength, mobility, anatomy and injury history all limit what a given pitcher can or should change.
- Changes to arm path, arm angle or effort level, and adding a new pitch, change the load on the arm [19]. Make them with context:
  - the pitcher's current workload and pitch counts;
  - his throwing and injury history;
  - the plan agreed with the rest of the staff, the strength coach and medical/athletic-training staff.
- MLB and USA Baseball's **Pitch Smart** guidelines cover pitchers aged 7–22. They set daily pitch limits and required rest, no pitching three days in a row, and at least three months a year without high-intensity throwing. They also note that the limits cover games only [24].
- Many academy players are 16–22, so these guidelines apply directly.
- **No cue in this file is universally correct.** Lessons should model a coach *proposing* a change and checking how it feels, not giving orders.

## 4. How coaches actually communicate

**Coaching principles, with the evidence behind them:**
- **External focus usually beats internal focus.** A 2021 set of meta-analyses (Chua, Wulf and colleagues) covered 73 performance studies and 40 learning studies. External focus (on the ball, the target or the effect) outperformed internal focus (on body parts) for performance, retention and transfer. Age, health and skill level did not change the result [20].
  - Example: "Throw it through the catcher's glove" rather than "Extend your elbow."
  - In practice, coaches of elite youth pitchers gave mostly internal-focus instructions: only 31% of focus statements were external [22].
  - One small thesis with 11 skilled pitchers found no accuracy benefit, so the evidence for pitching specifically is thinner.
- **Autonomy and expectancy.**
  - The OPTIMAL theory of motor learning (Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016) names three factors that support learning: an external focus, learner **autonomy** (giving the athlete choices) and **enhanced expectancies** (confidence that he can succeed) [21].
  - The theory is debated: critics question how far autonomy and expectancy effects reach.
  - These are the research bases for **asking before telling** and for building player buy-in.
- **One cue at a time; one objective per set of throws (unsourced: general coaching usage).** Widely taught, and consistent with keeping attention on a single external goal, but not a tested rule in the sources found here.
- **Feel + data.** Ask what he felt first, then show the number or the video, then link the two [23].

**Communication moves to model in lessons** (all **unsourced: general coaching usage**; the Spanish is in the term library):

1. **Ask what the pitcher felt:** "How did that one feel?" / "What did you feel in your fingers?"
2. **Suggest a small grip change:** "Let's try moving your thumb a little under the ball."
3. **Demonstrate finger placement:** "Put your middle finger here, on the seam. Like this."
4. **Describe how the ball moved:** "That one ran more to your arm side." / "It dropped late."
5. **Compare two pitches:** "That changeup looked like your fastball until the end, good." / "This one had more spin than the last one."
6. **Correct a mechanical issue without blaming:** "Your foot landed a bit closed. Let's aim it at the catcher."
7. **Give bullpen feedback:** short, positive and specific. "Good one. One more just like that."
8. **Ask whether a change feels natural:** "Does this grip feel natural, or forced?"
9. **Set one objective for a set of throws:** "These next five: just focus on the fastball location down and away."
10. **Use video together:** "Let's look at the video together."

## 5. Spanish in Latin American / Dominican baseball

**Loanwords and code-switching are normal.**
- Dominican and wider Caribbean baseball Spanish mixes adapted English with Spanish.
- Spanish-language MLB and Dominican press use these loanwords as ordinary words, without translating them:
  - **slider** [28][38] and **sweeper** [29][48];
  - **sinker** [26][50] and **cutter** [38];
  - **splitter** [31];
  - **bullpen**: "sesión de bullpen" [37], "lanzará desde el bullpen" (El Nuevo Diario, via search);
  - **strike** in "la zona de strike" [40];
  - **coach**: "coach de lanzadores" [36], "coach de pitcheo".
- Spanish words sit next to them:
  - **la recta** [25], also **la bola rápida** [34];
  - **el cambio (de velocidad)** [27];
  - **la curva** [47];
  - **la recta de cuatro/dos costuras** [25][26];
  - **la herradura** [33];
  - **el agarre** [32];
  - **el quiebre** [31];
  - **el ángulo del brazo** [30];
  - **el montículo** [39][52];
  - **la recta cortada** for the cutter, listed separately from the slider [29].
- **Gender of loanwords varies.** "El slider" and "el sinker" are usual: MLB.com en español writes *"el sinker"* throughout [50], and Diario Libre writes *"el slider"* [51]. ESPN Deportes once wrote *"una sinker"* (search excerpt). Teach the masculine and mention the variation.

**Press usage checked with curl (November 2026).** Each phrase below appeared in the full text of the article cited:
- **MLB.com en español, Eury Pérez [50]:**
  - *"su particular sinker de dos costuras"*;
  - *"la recta de cuatro costuras de tres dígitos"*;
  - *"el sinker con mayor tasa de rotación"*;
  - *"su ángulo del brazo casi lateral"*.
- **MLB.com en español, Sandy Alcántara [29]:**
  - a repertoire of *"sinker, cambio, recta de cuatro costuras, slider y recta cortada"*;
  - *"estrenó su slider tipo sweeper … durante su primera sesión del bullpen"*;
  - *"ajustar el agarre"*, using the idea of throwing it *"como una curva"*.
- **MLB.com en español, Andrés Muñoz [49]:**
  - *"un cambio con mucha 'caída'"*;
  - *"sostiene la pelota … con su pulgar derecho y su dedo índice formando un círculo mientras levanta su dedo medio"*;
  - *"un agarre firme de sus dedos índice y medio"*;
  - *"sesiones de bullpen"*.
- **Diario Libre (Dominican Republic):**
  - *"una recta a 103.9 mph"*, *"otro rectazo"*, *"un slider a 89.6 mph"*, *"buscaba el slider"* [51];
  - *"ha subido al montículo"*, *"la zona de strike"* [52];
  - *"una bola rápida sobre las 100 mph"* [53];
  - *"subió a la colina de los sustos"*, a columnist's flourish for the mound [54];
  - *"su recta cortante de 100 mph"* (Emmanuel Clase) [55].
- **Not confirmed:**
  - *"la loma"* (mound) and *"la rápida"* (fastball) did not turn up in any page read. Search excerpts showed only "montículo", "colina" and "bola rápida", so both stay unverified.
  - Listín Diario, El Nuevo Diario and El Día pages returned no readable text to curl.

**Spelling and the verb.**
- The RAE records **pichar** and **pichear** ("to pitch") and **pícher** [41].
- The Dominican Fundéu Guzmán Ariza notes these are written **without a "t"** (pícher, pichar, picheo) because they are established Spanish words. It also notes *beisbol* stressed on the last syllable is the usual Dominican pronunciation [43].
- Dominican press uses **picheo** [34]. MLB.com en español prefers **pitcheo** and **lanzamiento**.
- The *Diccionario del español dominicano* gives *pichar* the baseball sense first, but it also has other, colloquial senses [44]. "Pichar" outside baseball can mean dealing with, or ignoring, someone. Coaches should stick to **tirar** and **lanzar**, which are safe everywhere.

**Field shorthand vs formal/technical usage.**

| Concept | Field shorthand (what players say) | Formal / press / technical |
|---|---|---|
| fastball | la recta; (unverified) la rápida | la bola rápida [53], recta de cuatro costuras [50]; *rectazo* (a hard fastball) [51] |
| pitch (n.) | el pitcheo / picheo, el tiro | el lanzamiento |
| to pitch/throw | tirar, pichar | lanzar |
| pitcher | el pícher | el lanzador; press: el serpentinero [35] |
| mound | la loma (unverified for DR) | el montículo [39][52]; *la colina* (press flourish) [54]; DLE: *lomita* in Cuba, Nicaragua, Puerto Rico, Venezuela [42] |
| home plate | el plato, el home | el plato |
| spin | el spin, la rotación | la rotación, *tasa de rotación* (spin rate) [50], las RPM [49] |
| release | el release, soltar la bola | el punto de liberación / de salida (unverified which is standard) |
| movement / break | se mueve, rompe, cae | el movimiento, el quiebre [31], la caída [49] |
| mechanics | la mecánica | la mecánica |

- Words such as **serpentinero** and **lanzallamas** ("flamethrower") [25][35] are press words. Coaches recognise them but rarely use them to a player's face.

**Tú vs usted.** No sociolinguistic study of Dominican coach–player address was found. Studies of other Spanish-speaking regions show *usted* is chosen mainly by the listener's age and status, for respect, distance or hierarchy (search summaries; not Dominican data). Working guidance, **not source-verified**:
- With teenage academy players, an adult coach normally uses **tú**.
- Use **usted**, or ask, with older or veteran players, staff, parents and older Dominican adults.
- Players may well call the coach *usted*, or simply "coach", even when he says *tú*.
- Lessons should teach both forms of every command: *pon / ponga*, *suelta / suelte*.

**Expressions a coach will hear** (only those with some source):
- **"Estás ponchao"** means "you're in trouble". It comes from *ponchar* (to strike out) and is used across the Caribbean and parts of Central America. BBC Mundo also lists **"estar en tres y dos"** for being in a tight spot [46].
- **ampaya** means umpire; it is the form used in the DR and Cuba [45].
- **la recta**, **el cambio**, **la curva**, **el sinker**, **el slider**, **la recta cortada**: see above.
- No source was found in this session for other Dominican expressions often cited online, such as *"la rápida"*, *"la loma"*, *"tirar duro"*, *"tiene brazo"* or *"vaina"*. They are probably common. Have a Dominican speaker confirm them before teaching.

**Literal translations to avoid** (unsourced reasoning, flagged for native review):
- **"carry" → "llevar"** and **"ride" → "montar"** do not work. Use *"la recta sube / se mantiene arriba"*, or the loanwords *carry* / *ride*, which already appear in MLB.com en español [30].
- **"break" → "romper"** is acceptable. In technical talk *"el quiebre"* is better, and so is *"se cae"* for drop.
- **"bola" is ambiguous**: it is both "ball" (the object) and "ball" (the count). Use **la pelota** for the object when it matters.
- **"stride" → "zancada"** is correct but formal. Players may simply say *"el paso"*.
- **"feel" → "sentir" works**, and *"¿cómo te sentiste?"* is natural. Avoid *"¿cómo sentiste?"*, which needs an object: *"¿cómo sentiste la pelota?"*.
- **"pitch"** as a noun can't always be *"lanzamiento"* in field talk. Players often say *"el pitcheo"* or name the pitch.
