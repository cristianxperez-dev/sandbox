THREE UNITS, ONE INSTINCT  [00:00:00]

Here's a scene you may have lived through in some form. Three units in a large hospital system all post poor hand-hygiene audit results in the same quarter. The chief nursing officer's first instinct is to find the careless nurses and retrain them. Hold on to that instinct. We're going to take it apart. First, notice the assumption inside that instinct: that the cause lives inside individual people. Now add three facts. This is a hypothetical composite, but you may recognize the pattern. In the first unit, the sanitizer dispensers sit at the far end of every room. In the second, a respected senior physician routinely skips hand hygiene, and nobody says a word. In the third, audits are announced in advance everywhere except on night shift. Every one of those facts is a feature of the situation. And each one predicts the pattern of failures better than any personality inventory would. So here's a quick decision before we go any further.

THE OBSERVER'S LOPSIDED VIEW  [00:00:59]

If you chose to look at the role, you followed the rule this lesson is built on. A problem that keeps recurring across different people in the same role is evidence about the role. So why is that so hard to see? Because observers are systematically lopsided. Social psychologists call it the fundamental attribution error. We explain other people's behavior by their dispositions, their character, ability or attitude, and we underweight the pressures acting on them. You'll also hear it called correspondence bias. A word of care. The term is standard, but how universal and how large the tendency is has been debated. Treat it as a reliable default, not a law. Here's the structure underneath it. When you miss a deadline, you see everything around you: the competing demands, the late data, the unclear brief. When a colleague misses one, you see the colleague. Situational forces are often invisible from outside. Incentives, informal norms, who's in the room, what happened the last time someone raised a concern. The lesson offers a simple diagnostic grid with four levers. It's a teaching device, not an empirical taxonomy, but it earns its keep. First, incentives and measures. The misreading is: they only care about their numbers. The better question: what would a reasonable person do if paid exactly this way? Second, group norms. Not: that team has a culture problem. Instead: what happens to the person who behaves differently? Third, status and audience. Not: he folds under pressure. Instead: who was in the room, and what did dissent cost last time? Fourth, friction and defaults. Not: they don't follow the process. Instead: how many steps does compliance take, compared with the shortcut? Go back to the hospital. The physician is a norm. The distant dispensers are friction. The announced audits are a measure. Three levers, and no careless nurses required.

CONFORMITY: REAL, BUT VARIABLE  [00:02:57]

The most studied demonstration of situational power is Solomon Asch's line-judgment task. A participant sits with a group of confederates. On some trials, they all give the same obviously wrong answer about which line matches a reference line. And many participants go along with the group on at least some trials. The question for an executive isn't whether that's dramatic. It's what the accumulated evidence shows. Rod Bond and Peter Smith pooled studies that used Asch's task in a meta-analysis. Their first conclusion: conformity is real and repeatable. Their second: its size varies. It rises with the size of the majority. It's larger in more collectivist cultures. And in U.S. samples, it declined over the decades after Asch's original work. That combination, real but variable, is what most robust social findings look like. The direction generalizes. The rate does not. So let's be precise about what this evidence does and doesn't show. It does show that people shift toward unanimous groups, especially when they care about belonging to the group. It doesn't give you a rate you can carry into your boardroom. That depends on culture, era, stakes and how ambiguous the judgment is. Organizational judgments are usually far more ambiguous than the length of a line. That plausibly makes social influence stronger. Then again, dissent can be rewarded, which makes it weaker. Because the size depends on the setting, the sensible response is structural rather than exhortative. Try it on a case.

THE AUDIT  [00:04:33]

The written-estimates fix works because the meeting changes, not the people. Now for the uncomfortable half of the lesson. Social psychology built its reputation on dramatic demonstrations. Over the last fifteen years, it has also become a leader in auditing itself. You need both the insight and the audit. First, a definition. A finding replicates when a new study, using the same procedure on new participants, produces a similar result. A direct replication repeats the method as closely as possible. A conceptual replication tests the same idea a different way. Direct replications are the stronger test, because conceptual ones can succeed for different reasons, or quietly go unpublished when they fail. Why would a published result fail to replicate? None of the common reasons requires fraud. Small samples produce noisy estimates. Flexibility in analysis lets researchers try several outcomes or exclusion rules and report the one that worked. And publication filters favor significant results. Put them together, and published effects are, on average, larger than the true effects. A famous result is not the same as a reliable one. In two thousand fifteen, the Open Science Collaboration repeated one hundred studies from three leading psychology journals, using high-powered designs and, where possible, the original materials. Most originals had reported significant results. Far fewer replications did, and the replication effects averaged roughly half the original estimates. Social psychology studies replicated less often than cognitive psychology studies. And one detail matters a great deal. Originals with larger effects and stronger statistical evidence were more likely to hold up. The strength of the original evidence is itself information. Then there is the cautionary tale every manager should know. Ego depletion is the idea that self-control draws on a limited resource that gets used up by earlier acts of self-control. It spread widely into leadership and productivity advice. A registered replication report had more than twenty laboratories run one preregistered protocol. The pooled effect was close to zero. So here's a working classification. It reflects the evidence summarized in this lesson, not a final verdict. Conformity: robust, and variable in size. Use it by collecting independent judgments before discussion. Over-attribution to character: a widely supported default, whose size varies by culture and prompt. Use it by investigating the role before the person. Ego depletion: failed a large preregistered multi-lab test. Do not build policy on it. And the general claim that published social-psychology effects are reliable at face value: not supported. Discount single classic studies, and look for meta-analyses and preregistered replications. ARGUING WITH THE AUDIT  [00:07:39]

If you caught the word always, good. Now let me argue against the audit itself. The replication debate isn't settled in every detail. Critics of the two thousand fifteen project argued that some replications differed from the originals in population or procedure, and that a single replication can't establish that an effect is false. Proponents of ego depletion argued that the multi-lab protocol used too weak a manipulation. Those objections deserve a hearing. But they cut both ways. If an effect appears only under narrow conditions that its proponents can't specify in advance, it isn't a reliable basis for designing organizations. And the broader conclusion, that published effects are often inflated and some celebrated ones aren't real, has held across later many-laboratory projects. One more precision. A preregistered null is strong evidence that the standard ego-depletion paradigm doesn't produce a meaningful effect. It's weaker evidence about every possible version of self-control fatigue. What you need to know is that the confident popular version has no reliable support. There's also a mirror-image error, and smart leaders make it. Having learned that situations matter, some conclude that individual differences don't. That overcorrects. Ability, conscientiousness and expertise predict performance in many jobs, and selection decisions rest on that fact. Situations also select people. A sales floor with aggressive targets attracts and keeps people who are comfortable with them. The mature position is interactional. People differ, situations differ, and the same incentive or norm produces different behavior in different people. So when you inherit a puzzle, ask a comparison question. Does the pattern follow the person across roles, or the role across people? You will rarely get a clean answer, so decide what would change your mind before you look. And one last irony. Classic findings became famous partly because many people repeated them: textbooks, consultants and speakers citing each other. Popularity is a social signal, not an evidential one. Let's bring it together. Observers over-attribute behavior to character, so a problem that recurs across people in the same role is evidence about the role. Conformity is real and repeatable, but its size depends on majority size, culture, era and ambiguity. Design meetings to capture independent views first. And the replication record is mixed. Some classics hold, many effects are smaller than published, and ego depletion failed a rigorous test. Diagnose the role before the person, and check the evidence before you quote the study.
