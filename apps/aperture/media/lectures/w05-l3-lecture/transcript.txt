When Feedback Makes People Worse
Coaching, feedback and managing through others

A PREDICTION ABOUT FEEDBACK  [00:00:00]

Most managers believe two things about feedback: it is reliably good, and more is better. Today I want to test the first belief, and then show you where a senior leader’s real leverage lies. Imagine researchers gathering every study they can find in which people got feedback on their performance, and comparing them with similar people who got none. Across all those studies, what share of feedback interventions made performance worse than no feedback at all? Most managers guess something small, under one in ten. Make your own guess.

WHERE ATTENTION GOES  [00:00:34]

Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi ran that review and meta-analysis. Feedback improved performance on average. But results were highly variable, and more than a third of interventions reduced performance. A positive average with a wide spread means some interventions helped a lot and others hurt. So the useful question is not does feedback work. It is what separates the ones that work from the ones that backfire. Their answer is feedback intervention theory. We regulate behavior by comparing performance with standards, and attention is limited. A feedback message says performance differs from a standard. Then attention moves, among the details of the task, the motivation to do it, and the self: identity, competence, standing. If attention stays on the task and on how to close the gap, effort and strategy adjust, and performance tends to improve. If attention moves to the self, energy goes to protecting self-image. People defend, discount the source, or disengage, and performance can fall. A hypothetical radiology department posted a monthly ranking of report turnaround. Some improved, but careful readers began to rush, and the talk shifted to who was at the bottom. The feedback was accurate. What it drew attention to was status. Hear the difference. Self-focused: you’re disorganized. You’re not a strong storyteller. Task-focused: two pending labs weren’t in the handoff, use the checklist before you leave. The recommendation is on slide fourteen, move it to slide one. Notice that praise can be self-focused too. You’re a natural tells someone nothing to repeat. Your summary let the committee decide in ten minutes because you gave the trade-off first tells them exactly what worked. Now try it yourself.

THE HARD CONVERSATION  [00:02:28]

That kind of sentence lives inside a conversation. Here is a five-step structure. It is a practitioner heuristic, not a tested protocol. Purpose: say why you are meeting, and what you want for the person. Observation: a specific, verifiable behavior, what you saw or heard, and when. Impact: the effect on the work or on others, without labeling the person. Inquiry: ask for their view and listen. You may be missing context. Agreement: a specific next behavior, and a date to review it. Defensiveness is the predictable response when a message threatens identity. You can’t prevent it, but you can lower the threat: keep observations specific, don’t bury the message under sweeteners, and allow silence after the hard sentence. If someone says, so you think I’m the problem, separate identity from behavior. Your analysis is the best on the team. I’m talking about one behavior in reviews that is costing you influence. The same logic holds on a practice field. A player told you lack mental toughness gets feedback about the self. A player told after a two-strike foul your setup widens, let’s rehearse resetting your stance, gets feedback about the task. And a coordinator who corrects a coach in front of players makes the correction about status. And narrow it. In a hypothetical agency, a manager told her contractor oversight is weak arrives with documentation of every meeting. What moves the conversation is one gap: two invoices approved without the deliverable check. Ask what made that check hard. Often the answer is a system problem the leader can fix.

MANAGING THROUGH OTHERS  [00:04:11]

Now the bigger lever. Managing through others is mostly design, not conversation. How you delegate and follow up shapes more than any single feedback talk. Delegation runs along a continuum. This version is a practitioner heuristic, a synthesis of common management texts, not an empirically calibrated instrument. Level one, tell: do it this way. For a novice in this domain, with high stakes and no time. Level two, consult: give me your recommendation, I decide. Useful when you are building judgment. Level three, agree: decide, but check with me before acting. Sound reasoning, stakes still high. Level four, act and report. A track record in this domain, and reversible decisions. Level five, own: it’s yours, I’ll ask about results quarterly, and you will not second-guess. The right level depends on demonstrated readiness in this specific kind of decision, and on the cost of an error. Here is where it goes wrong. The workshop case is invented, but you will recognize it. Accountability, in this frame, is an agreement about what success looks like, who decides, and when results are discussed, made before the work starts. The most common error at senior levels is not harshness. It is ambiguity, followed by disappointment. Player development has both layers. Coaches coach players, and coordinators coach coaches. An affiliate coach who thinks he owns a player’s plan, while the organization thinks he is executing it, will feel every adjustment from above as distrust.

WHAT COACHING EVIDENCE SHOWS  [00:05:51]

Coaching is a longer relationship, with feedback as one ingredient among goal setting, questioning and practice. Rebecca Jones, Stephen Woods and Yves Guillaume meta-analyzed workplace coaching and found positive average effects on learning and performance outcomes, varying by outcome and arrangement. Now the challenge to everything in this lecture. The coaching literature is small relative to the industry, and many studies rely on self-report. Selection: coaching goes to high-potentials or people in trouble, so the groups differ before it starts. Self-report: the coachee, who invested time and may like the coach, often rates the result. No placebo: any attention from a senior person may help. And a publication filter: positive results from providers are more likely to be written up. The feedback meta-analysis has limits too. It drew heavily on laboratory and narrow tasks, without the careers and politics of a real conversation with a senior employee. And neither literature tells you which coaching method works best. Two final nuances. Feedforward, asking for suggestions about the future rather than verdicts on the past, is a popular practitioner technique. Its logic fits the theory, since it keeps attention on the next task. But treat it as a plausible design idea with a thin evidence base, not an established finding. And purely task-focused feedback can turn cold. Some people need to hear that their standing is secure before they can attend to the task. The same sentence lands differently from a manager whose benevolence is trusted than from one whose benevolence is in doubt. That is last lesson’s trust model at work, and team-leader coaching was one of the structures linked to psychological safety.

SYNTHESIS  [00:07:46]

One last scene, to pull the pieces together. Your most productive direct report delivers outstanding work, and is often dismissive of colleagues in meetings. Two people have asked to move off her projects. There is no formal complaint, some colleagues privately admire her, and losing her would hurt a key deliverable. A clean answer does not exist. The task-focused path is to name specific behaviors and their costs, make an explicit agreement, and follow up, accepting the risk that she leaves. The alternative is to wait. But waiting is also a decision. It tells everyone else what the organization values. Feedback theory, the conversation structure and delegation clarity are not a script for this case. They are how you make the trade-off deliberately rather than by default. Let’s bring it together. Feedback helps on average but backfires often, and the difference lies largely in where it sends attention: toward the task and the next step, or toward the self. A hard conversation lowers threat with purpose, observation, impact, inquiry and agreement. Coaching shows positive average effects, from an imperfect literature. And managing through others depends on explicit delegation levels and accountability agreements, made before the work starts. Take the comprehension check below, the same as the written lesson.
