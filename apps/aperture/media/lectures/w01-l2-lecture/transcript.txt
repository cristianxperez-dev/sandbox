THE SCREENING PUZZLE  [00:00:00]

This is a lecture about a puzzle that has embarrassed some of the most capable professionals in the world. Not because they lack intelligence, but because the mind they bring to the problem was built for a different job. We'll start with a number, and I'm going to ask you to commit to it before I explain anything. A hospital network is evaluating a new screening test for a serious condition. In the population being screened, about one person in a hundred has the condition. The test catches ninety percent of the people who have it. And among people who do not have it, the test wrongly flags nine percent. A patient tests positive. What is the probability that this patient actually has the condition? If you said something like eighty or ninety percent, you reasoned the way most people do. The correct answer is about nine percent. That is not a trick. The numbers are invented for the lesson, but the structure is the classic base-rate problem, and it appears every time a rare event meets an imperfect signal. A fraud alert, a security screening, a drug test, a scouting report on a player who looks like a future star. Same structure, same trap. To see why the trap works, we need to look at the shortcuts the mind uses when it estimates likelihood.

THREE SHORTCUTS  [00:01:16]

In 1974 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a short paper in Science that changed how we think about judgment. Their claim was that when people judge probabilities, they quietly substitute an easier question for the hard one. The first substitute is representativeness. Instead of asking how likely something is, we ask how much it resembles the category we have in mind. A positive test looks like disease. A candidate who looks like our past stars looks like success. Resemblance feels like probability, and it pushes the base rate out of view. The second is availability. We judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind. After one dramatic fraud case, an audit committee will spend its attention on that fraud type, while quieter and more frequent risks go unexamined. The third is anchoring and adjustment. We start from whatever number is in front of us and move away from it, but not far enough. Their most famous demonstration used a rigged wheel of fortune. Participants watched the wheel stop on a number, and were then asked what percentage of African countries were members of the United Nations. People who saw the wheel stop at ten gave a median estimate of twenty-five percent. People who saw it stop at sixty-five gave a median estimate of forty-five percent. Everyone knew the wheel was random. The number still pulled the estimate toward it. Tversky and Kahneman were careful to say these shortcuts are economical and usually effective. The problem is that their failures are systematic, so they are predictable, and that means we can design around them. Let's test whether you can spot one in the wild.

SEEING THE BASE RATE  [00:03:00]

Now let's solve the screening problem in a way that makes the answer almost obvious. Imagine one thousand people walking through the screening. About ten of them have the condition. Nine hundred and ninety do not. The test catches nine of the ten who are ill. But it also flags nine percent of the healthy people, and nine percent of nine hundred and ninety is about eighty-nine. So roughly ninety-eight people test positive, and only nine of them are actually ill. Nine out of ninety-eight is about nine percent. Nothing about the problem changed. Only the format did. Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed this experimentally in 1995. When the same Bayesian problems were presented as natural frequencies, counts of cases from one reference population, people without statistical training reached the correct answer far more often, in up to about half of their inferences. Counts keep the base rate in front of you. Percentages let it slip away. The practical lesson is a habit of mind, and a habit of design. When someone brings you a compelling case, a candidate, a deal, an alert, ask first: of all the cases that look like this, how many turn out to be real? And when you build dashboards and reports, write them in counts. A clinical system that says the model is ninety percent sensitive invites base-rate neglect. One that says, of every hundred patients flagged, about nine will have the condition, changes what people do with the alert.

LOOKING FOR WHAT YOU WANT TO FIND  [00:04:31]

So far we have looked at errors that happen even when we have no stake in the answer. Now we turn to the two habits that do the most damage to anyone who evaluates people, projects or evidence. The first is confirmation bias. Raymond Nickerson's review defines it as seeking or interpreting evidence in ways that are partial to beliefs we already hold. A classic demonstration is Peter Wason's rule-discovery task. You are told that the sequence two, four, six follows a rule, and you may propose other sequences to find out what the rule is. Suppose your hypothesis is: numbers that go up by two. In the task, people overwhelmingly propose sequences that fit their own hypothesis, get a yes every time, and announce the wrong rule with confidence. The actual rule is just: any three ascending numbers. You only discover it by testing a case your hypothesis says should fail. Nickerson stresses that none of this requires bad faith. It is how attention works when it is guided by a hypothesis. The second habit is motivated reasoning, and it adds a motive. Ziva Kunda distinguished accuracy goals, which make us process information more carefully, from directional goals, which steer us toward a conclusion we already want. Her crucial observation was that directional reasoning is constrained. We reach the conclusion we want only when we can build a justification that would persuade a neutral observer. That is how we keep the feeling of being objective. Put the two ideas together and you get a simple map. Without a stake and with an open search, our errors come mostly from the heuristics we have already met. A hypothesis narrows the search: that is confirmation bias, and the remedy is to test what would prove you wrong. A personal stake steers the search: the remedy is to separate the person who champions a decision from the person who reviews it, and to state the criteria before the evidence arrives. And when both are present, when you designed the program and you are also judging it, you are in the most dangerous cell of all. Kunda's constraint is the lever for a leader. Raise the bar for what counts as a justification, and directional reasoning loses room to move.

IS “BIAS” THE RIGHT WORD?  [00:06:48]

Now I want to challenge the story I have just told you. There is a serious alternative reading of this evidence, the ecological-rationality program led by Gerd Gigerenzer. On this view, heuristics are not glitches. They are strategies that deliberately ignore information, and whether they work depends on the environment they are used in. Gigerenzer and Wolfgang Gaissmaier argue that under high uncertainty and small samples, simple rules can predict as well as, or better than, complex models, because a complex model fitted to a little noisy data learns the noise along with the signal. So calling a heuristic a bias assumes the laboratory problem is the right yardstick. Tversky and Kahneman themselves described the shortcuts as usually effective. Much of the disagreement is about emphasis, and about which standard of good judgment to apply. Which raises a fair question for a skeptical executive: how solid are these findings anyway? A coordinated replication project tested thirteen classic effects across thirty-six samples in many countries. Ten replicated consistently, anchoring among them. Two social-priming effects did not. And the differences between labs, countries and online versus in-person settings were small compared with the differences between effects. The lesson is selective trust, not blanket belief or blanket dismissal. One last point, and it is the one I most want you to keep. Knowing about a bias does not reliably protect you from it. People see bias easily in others and rarely in themselves. That is why every remedy in this lesson is a procedure rather than an exhortation. Ask for the base rate before the story. Present evidence as counts. Give the review to someone without a stake. State the criteria before the evidence arrives. And assign someone to build the strongest case against the option everyone likes. A procedure works whether or not the person following it feels biased. Let's bring it together.
