The Feeling of Knowing
Intuition, deliberation and when to trust your gut

THREE PEOPLE WHO JUST KNEW  [00:00:00]

Every experienced leader has had the moment. You look at a situation for a few seconds, and you simply know. This lecture is about that feeling. Where it comes from, when it deserves your trust, and when it is telling you nothing at all. Let's begin with three people who all had it. These are hypothetical composites, but they are the kinds of cases the research on intuition was built on. First, a fireground commander. She orders her crew out of a burning house moments before the floor collapses. Afterwards she cannot fully say why. Second, a senior radiologist. He glances at a chest film and says, that is a tumor, before the formal read has even started. Third, a veteran portfolio manager. He feels strongly that a stock is about to recover, and he doubles the position. All three are fast. All three are fluent. All three are confident. And all three would describe the experience in the same words: I just knew. So here's your first decision. Which of these three intuitions deserves the least trust? And notice: you can't use experience or confidence to separate them, because they all have plenty of both. If you put the portfolio manager last, you already sense where this lecture is going. The feeling of knowing was the same in all three. What differs is the warrant behind it. Fire behavior and medical images have regularities that thousands of repetitions, with feedback, can teach. Short-term stock prices offer very few. Same confidence, very different grounds. To see why, we first need to clear away a popular map of the mind that gets in the way. You've almost certainly heard it in a boardroom.

WHAT THE TWO-SYSTEMS STORY CLAIMS  [00:01:40]

The popular version says we have two systems. System one is fast and intuitive. System two is slow and deliberate. And, the slogan adds, system one is biased and system two is rational. The researchers who defend dual-process theory most carefully, Jonathan Evans and Keith Stanovich, say something more precise. They talk about two types of processing. Type one processing is autonomous. It runs whenever its triggering conditions are present, and it does not need working memory. Type two processing does need working memory. Working memory is your small mental workspace, the place where you hold a phone number while you dial it, or compare two job offers on three dimensions at once. It is scarce, and anything that loads it, an interruption, a meeting that runs over, reduces your capacity for type two thinking. What type two buys you is hypothetical thinking: the ability to hold a what-if separately from what you currently believe, and reason about it. Evans and Stanovich call this cognitive decoupling. Here's the distinction that most popular accounts skip. On one side are the defining features: autonomy for type one, working memory and hypothetical thinking for type two. On the other side are the features that usually travel with each type. Fast versus slow. Unconscious versus conscious. Associative versus rule-based. And, crucially, biased versus correct. Evans and Stanovich insist those are correlates, not definitions. Type one is often fast and often unconscious. But it is not defined by being biased. And type two is not defined by being right. Think about what that means in practice. Deliberate reasoning can be slow, careful and wrong. It can build a sophisticated rationalization for a conclusion you already wanted. So the better reading is this. Intuition is accurate where it was trained on valid cues, and error-prone where it was not. Deliberation is necessary for novel problems, and it can rationalize as easily as it can correct.

IS THE NUMBER TWO A MYTH?  [00:03:46]

Now, an honest complication. Dual-process theory is not settled science. David Melnikoff and John Bargh argue that the two-type framework is a myth. Their paper is called The Mythical Number Two. Their case is that the features said to define each type, unintentional versus intentional, uncontrollable versus controllable, efficient versus effortful, unconscious versus conscious, frequently come apart in real mental processes. If the features do not cluster, sorting processes into two bins misdescribes them, and, they argue, slows scientific progress. Better to study the specific features directly. Evans and Stanovich anticipated part of this attack. Their reply: we define the types by working memory and autonomy only, not by a bundle of features that all line up. So critics who show misaligned features are, in their view, attacking a version of the theory they do not hold. The debate is live, and I am not going to pretend it has been resolved. So what should a decision-maker take from a debate they cannot referee? Something modest. There is good evidence that some thinking is effortful and capacity-limited, some is not, and the two can conflict. There is much weaker support for the strong popular claims: that the mind contains two separate systems, that intuition is the source of error and deliberation the cure, or that engaging system two is a reliable way to remove bias. In the next lesson you'll see deliberation amplify error when it's used to defend a conclusion. So when you hear system one, system two in a meeting, treat it as a loose description, not as a mechanism and not as a remedy. Which leaves the question that actually matters for leaders. Not which system is better, but when has an intuition earned trust?

WHEN INTUITION EARNS TRUST  [00:05:37]

The most useful answer came from an unusual collaboration. Daniel Kahneman had spent a career documenting the errors of intuition. Gary Klein had spent his documenting the skilled intuition of firefighters, nurses and military commanders. They wrote a joint paper to find where they agreed. Their shared answer is that intuition is a form of recognition. Patterns stored through long experience. And a skilled intuition is trustworthy only when two conditions hold. First, the environment must have high validity. There have to be stable relationships between the cues you can observe and the outcomes that follow. Second, the person must have had an adequate opportunity to learn those cues: prolonged practice, with feedback that is timely and clear. Both conditions. Not one. A regular world you never got feedback on teaches you nothing. Endless practice in an unpredictable world teaches you the wrong thing. Then Kahneman and Klein add a warning that runs against ordinary experience. Subjective confidence is not a reliable indicator of accuracy. People feel just as sure in environments where nothing can be predicted as in environments where a great deal can. Why? Because the feeling comes from the fluency of a coherent story, not from a record of being right. An intuition can be fluent, confident and worthless. That is why you couldn't use confidence to rank our three professionals. It was never going to discriminate. Put the two conditions on two axes and you get a map. It's APERTURE's summary of Kahneman and Klein, not their own diagram. Top left: a regular environment, but the person has not seen enough cases. The cues exist; the experience does not. Pair them with a checklist or a model, and build feedback loops. Top right: skilled intuition. Experienced fire commanders, chess masters, anesthesiologists, an infield coach reading a swing. Here, trust the judgment, and spot-check the edge cases. Bottom left: confident noise. Picking individual stocks, long-range political forecasts, predicting which sixteen-year-old becomes a star. Here you want base rates, models and independent judgments. And bottom right, the subtle one. Lots of practice on outcomes that are not predictable. The repetitions build confidence, not skill. Watch for the illusion of validity. Let's place a harder case.

THINKING ABOUT YOUR OWN THINKING  [00:08:04]

Exactly the move you just made has a name. Metacognition: monitoring and regulating your own thinking. It does not mean distrusting every intuition. It means asking, before you act on one, where it came from. Here is a version you can run in about thirty seconds. Notice. Name the judgment and how sure you feel, and write it down before anyone discusses it. Source. Is this a domain where you have seen many cases with clear, timely feedback? Validity. Is the outcome predictable from the available cues at all, by anyone? Route. If both answers are yes, act and log it. If either is no, find a base rate, a model or an independent second judgment. Review. Later, record what happened and compare it with what you predicted. Here's how the check plays out in one field: player development. The case is generic, no real club. An experienced hitting coach watches hundreds of swings a week and sees the batted-ball result immediately. That read sits near the top right of our map. The same evaluator projecting how a teenager will develop over five years gets feedback slowly, confounded by everything else in his development. Same person. Skilled in the first, merely confident in the second. A sensible department labels each evaluation by its horizon and feedback quality, and treats long-horizon intuitions as one input to combine with base rates and other independent reads. The same split runs through an emergency department. Recognizing on sight that a patient is sick is well practiced. An atypical presentation that rarely gets clear feedback is where a structured score or a second look adds most.

LIMITS, AND KEEPING SCORE  [00:09:47]

Now let me push against my own argument. High validity and adequate opportunity to learn are easy to say. They're hard to measure. Few organizations know how predictive their evaluators really are, because they rarely write predictions down and compare them with outcomes. Kahneman and Klein describe the conditions. They do not hand you a dial to read. And anecdotes cannot build that dial. Showing a skilled intuition takes three things: predictions recorded before the outcome, a comparison against a base rate or a simple model, and enough cases to separate skill from luck. Notice the asymmetry. Stories of great calls are cheap. Denominators are expensive. An organization that celebrates great calls without counting all the calls is measuring confidence, not skill. One practical route, if you have a respected senior voice you cannot simply overrule: make it procedural, not personal. Everyone, you included, writes long-horizon calls down with a probability before discussion, and reviews them a year later. If his intuition is excellent, the record will show it. Let's bring it together. Dual-process language describes a real contrast between autonomous and effortful thinking. But the slogan, intuition errs and deliberation corrects, is not supported, and whether there are two types at all is still debated. The test for trusting an intuition is about the environment: a regular, predictable domain, and an adequate opportunity to learn it with clear feedback. Confidence is not evidence.
