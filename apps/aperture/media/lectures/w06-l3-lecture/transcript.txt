Coalitions, legitimacy and the politics of change

ONE FRONT DOOR  [00:00:00]

This lecture is about why reorganizations that everyone announces so confidently so often exist only on paper. It is tempting to file that under bureaucracy. Organizational sociology offers something sharper: two theories about why change stalls, and one classic study about the hidden price of the most common remedy. A state government announces that three agencies, workforce training, vocational rehabilitation and adult education, will become one department, to give residents one front door. The case is hypothetical. Eighteen months later, the department exists on paper, with one logo. But the three former agencies still run separate intake systems, report to separate legislative committees, draw on separate federal funding streams, and hold separate staff meetings. The director who championed it has left. A long-serving deputy explains it in one sentence: nobody asked the people who had to give something up. Hold that sentence. It is almost a summary of the first theory.

THE ORGANIZATION AS A COALITION  [00:01:07]

Richard Cyert and James March, an economist and a political scientist, challenged the picture of the firm as a single profit-maximizing decision maker. In A Behavioral Theory of the Firm, they described the organization as a coalition: managers, workers, owners, specialists, with goals that differ. The organization’s goals come out of bargaining among them. The bargaining is never complete, so conflict is never fully resolved. It is held in check partly by attending to different goals at different times. And partly by side payments, budget, status, autonomy, policy commitments, that keep members in the coalition. And by organizational slack: surplus resources that let the organization pay several members at once. That is why slack makes change easier. You can compensate losers without taking from someone else. In this view organizations aim for satisfactory rather than optimal outcomes, and search for new solutions mainly when performance falls below aspiration. Later theorists spoke of the dominant coalition: the members whose agreement actually determines major decisions. Not the same thing as the top of the chart. So a change agent’s first question is not who is in charge. It is: what is the current settlement, who is party to it, and what does each party receive for staying in? A proposal reallocates some of those payments, often without saying so. Members who lose, or fear losing, see the bargain reopened. They resist: open objection, delay, minimal compliance, appeals to outside allies, like a legislative committee or a federal funder. The new settlement is stable only if enough members, with enough power, prefer it to the old one. That is what the merged department never reached. Here is the practical test of the coalition view. A long-serving manager is quietly slowing your reorganization. The coalition view suggests a more useful default than “resistance to change”. Some resistance is principled. It carries information the change agent lacks: workload invisible from above, commitments to outside partners, past attempts that failed for reasons no one recorded. Some is self-interested. But treating all of it as irrational wastes the information in the first kind and inflames the second.

WHY ORGANIZATIONS LOOK ALIKE  [00:03:39]

The second theory asks a different question: why do organizations in the same field look so alike? In everyday speech an institution is a big organization. In sociology it is a settled way of doing things that people take for granted, persisting because it seems legitimate, not only because it is efficient. Professional credentials, the budget cycle, the annual review, the idea that a hospital must have a quality department. And a field is the set of organizations that see each other as doing the same thing, with their regulators and professional bodies. Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell argued that once a field is established, organizations converge less because similarity is efficient than through institutional isomorphism. There are three mechanisms. Coercive isomorphism comes from pressure by those they depend on and by the state. A city adopts a performance dashboard because a federal grant requires its metrics. That example, like the others, is hypothetical. Mimetic isomorphism comes from uncertainty. When goals are ambiguous, organizations copy peers that look successful. A regional bank creates a chief innovation office after its rivals announce theirs. Normative isomorphism comes from professionalization. People trained in the same programs bring the same templates, so hospitals converge on similar quality methods. Conforming confers legitimacy: the perception that the organization is appropriate and proper. And legitimacy helps secure resources and support. Try one. Several peers of a mid-sized organization announce a new executive role. Shortly afterwards, the organization creates one too. No one analyzed whether it was needed. Mimetic, as you probably saw. And here is the risk DiMaggio and Powell’s framework predicts under mimetic and coercive pressure. Organizations adopt the visible form, the department, the dashboard, the title, while the underlying work continues unchanged. How would you know? Legitimacy-driven adoption follows peers and visibility more than evidence, and late adopters adopt in more ceremonial forms. Efficiency-driven adoption tracks expected benefit, and late adopters, having learned, implement more deeply. The trouble is that “peers adopted it” is also a rational signal, so the two are hard to tell apart. The usable lesson: when someone says you must adopt something because everyone has one, ask what evidence shows it changes outcomes, and design yours so its results can be checked, not merely displayed.

THE PRICE OF CO-OPTATION  [00:06:31]

So how do change agents usually deal with opponents? Often, they bring them inside. Philip Selznick’s nineteen forty-nine study of the Tennessee Valley Authority examined a federal agency committed to grass-roots administration, which worked through established local institutions and elites. He called the mechanism co-optation: absorbing new elements into an organization’s leadership or policy-making to avert threats to its stability or existence. Formal co-optation shares the symbols of participation. Informal co-optation shares real power with influential outside interests. And that is where Selznick’s uncomfortable finding lives. The interests brought inside gain leverage over the organization’s actual commitments. The support an agency buys can quietly redirect its programs away from its stated goals. The price of support can be the mission. Here is a hypothetical hospital. The quality officer wants every unit on a new handoff protocol. The surgical chair, whose surgeons dislike it, also chairs the committee that credentials physicians. She can co-opt him by asking him to lead the protocol’s adaptation for surgery: his support, at the price of letting surgery shape its own version. In any such case, map three things. The settlement: who receives what now, and what would the change take away? The legitimacy: which outside audiences will judge the change, and which templates will make it look proper? The price: if you co-opt an opponent or borrow a template, what control do you give away, and where is your line? Co-optation is sound when the co-opted party improves the design or influences only what you can concede. It is dangerous when it hands them the core purpose. Set that line in advance.

LIMITS, AND THE COALITION YOU INHERIT  [00:08:26]

Now, the case against everything I have said. The coalition view can make every decision look like a bargain among self-interested parties. Many professionals accept losses of budget or status because they believe a change is right. Institutional theory, in its strong form, predicts more sameness and less agency than we see. Some organizations break with their field and succeed, and some leaders create new institutions rather than conforming. And Selznick’s account is one historical case, richly interpreted. It is not a law about how often co-optation backfires. Use these as lenses for asking better questions, not as predictions that settle the answer. One last scenario. You take over a department in the middle of your predecessor’s change initiative. Several senior staff were promised roles in the new structure. You do not know exactly what was promised. Two are loud advocates; one is quietly slowing things down. Honoring every promise locks in a design you doubt. Breaking them signals that commitments here are worthless. Let’s bring it together. Organizations are coalitions held together by side payments and slack. Change reopens the settlement, and resistance is often a rational defense of it, carrying information you need. Fields reward legitimacy through coercive, mimetic and normative pressure, which can produce adoption in form only. And co-optation buys support by sharing control, at a price that can be the mission itself. Plan the coalition, the legitimacy and the price.
